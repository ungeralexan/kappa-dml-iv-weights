\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

This thesis studies classical kappa estimators of the local average treatment effect together with
machine-learning-assisted IV estimators, focusing on Wald-AIPW and PLR-IV. It combines \citet{abadie2003semiparametric} kappa framework with recent results on estimator
normalization and outcome weights to place both estimator classes within a common
diagnostic framework. 

A central contribution is the extension of translation-invariance analysis from
classical kappa estimators to complete data-adaptive estimation procedures. Rather than
evaluating only a fixed set of fitted weights, the analysis reruns nuisance estimation,
cross-fitting, and hyperparameter tuning after economically irrelevant changes in the
coding or units of the outcome. The empirical applications revisit the Vietnam draft
lottery, Card's college-proximity design, and the Angrist--Evans fertility design, and
compare normalized and unnormalized kappa estimators with PLR-IV and Wald-AIPW
implemented using several nuisance learners.

The empirical analysis examines whether normalization is reflected in
translation invariance and whether outcome-weight diagnostics reveal differences
in effective support, concentration, negative weights, and covariate balance that
are not visible from point estimates alone.

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} local average treatment effect, instrumental variables,
kappa weighting, outcome weights, translation invariance, covariate balance,
double machine learning.

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{JEL classification:} C14, C21, C26.

\section{Introduction}
Causal estimates should not depend on economically arbitrary choices in the coding of an outcome. Recording wages or income in cents rather than dollars before taking logarithms adds the constant $\log(100)$ to every observation but leaves the underlying economic comparison unchanged. An estimated effect on the logarithmic outcome should therefore remain unchanged. Nevertheless, \citet{sloczynski2025abadie} show that several consistent but unnormalized kappa estimators can respond strongly to such additive shifts. Normalization is therefore consequential because it determines whether an estimator is invariant to transformations that do not alter the economic content of the data.
This issue arises when instrument validity holds only after conditioning on observed covariates. Although applied work commonly relies on additive two-stage least squares, its interpretation as a LATE requires the covariate specification to capture the relevant conditional structure of instrument assignment \citep{sloczynski2021when,blandhol2026tsls}. The kappa theorem of \citet{abadie2003semiparametric} instead identifies complier moments through weights based on the conditional instrument propensity score. Its equivalent population representations generate sample estimators that can assign different coefficients to the observed outcomes and satisfy different normalization properties.
This thesis compares these classical kappa estimators with data-adaptive IV estimators constructed within the double machine learning framework of \citet{chernozhukov2018double}. Using the outcome-weight representation of \citet{knaus2024treatment}, each fitted estimator is examined through the observation-level coefficients that reproduce its estimate. This representation provides the basis for studying properties such as normalization or  effective support across the estimators and empirical designs considered below.











 
 
 
 
\subsection{Related literature}

The literature underlying this thesis developed from the identification of treatment effects for compliers towards increasingly flexible estimation and, more recently, the analysis of the observation-level weights implied by fitted causal estimators. \citet{angrist1995identification} and \citet{angrist1996identification} establish the conditions under which a binary instrument identifies the local average treatment effect for compliers when treatment effects are heterogeneous. \citet{imbens1997compliers} extend this framework beyond the complier mean to the marginal distributions of potential outcomes for compliers. Their analysis illustrates that instrumental-variable procedures can be understood through the weighted outcome distributions they implicitly construct. \citet{abadie2003semiparametric} subsequently develops a general semiparametric representation for moments of the complier population when instrument assignment is valid conditional on observed covariates. The resulting kappa weights recover these moments from observed data even though individual compliance types remain unobserved.\\
Subsequent work translated this conditional identification result into estimators that allow for flexible covariate adjustment. \citet{tan2006regression} develops regression and weighting procedures for instrumental-variable settings with covariates, while \citet{frolich2007nonparametric} derives a nonparametric LATE estimator based on a ratio of weighted reduced-form and first-stage contrasts. This unnormalized ratio became an important reference point in the literature, although weighting estimators of the LATE have remained uncommon in empirical applications \citep{sloczynski2025abadie}. \citet{uysal2011three} proposes a separately normalized version of the estimator. \\
Normalization has a longer history in survey sampling and inverse-probability weighting \citep{hajek1971comment,busso2014finite}. In conditional IV estimation, it is closely connected to the estimation of the instrument propensity score. Covariate-balancing approaches estimate propensity-score parameters by directly targeting balance conditions rather than relying exclusively on the fit of the instrument-assignment model \citep{imai2014covariate}. \citet{heiler2022efficient} transfers this principle to LATE estimation and constructs normalized estimators that achieve exact finite-sample balance across instrument groups. Building on these developments, \citet{sloczynski2025abadie} provide a unified analysis of five kappa-based LATE estimators. They show that only two of these estimators are fully normalized and distinguish normalized from unnormalized estimators through translation invariance and scale invariance for logarithmic outcomes. They further demonstrate that the separately normalized estimator proposed by \citet{uysal2011three} retains a positive denominator by construction under either form of one-sided noncompliance. Their theoretical results, simulations, and empirical applications motivate the use of this estimator as the principal normalized kappa benchmark in the present analysis.\\
A related development concerns the use of machine learning to estimate the conditional relationships required for causal and instrumental-variable estimation. \citet{chernozhukov2018double} introduce the double machine learning framework, which combines Neyman-orthogonal scores with cross-fitting to reduce the sensitivity of low-dimensional parameter estimates to regularization error and overfitting in estimated nuisance functions. Their framework covers both partially linear IV models and LATE estimation based on augmented scores. DML therefore permits more flexible adjustment than fixed parametric specifications, but its inferential theory does not by itself describe how the fitted procedure distributes weight across the observations in a particular sample.\\
A parallel literature examines this realized weighting structure. \citet{chattopadhyay2023implied} derive observation-level weights for linear regression estimators and use them to study covariate balance, representativeness, extrapolation, and effective support. \citet{knaus2024treatment} develops a general framework for deriving outcome weights from estimators represented through moment conditions. He applies the framework to prominent DML and generalized-random-forest estimators and shows that implementation choices determine whether an exact outcome-weight representation exists and which normalization properties it satisfies. The framework thereby extends diagnostics traditionally associated with explicit weighting estimators to multi-step causal machine-learning procedures.\\
The term ``weight'' refers to different objects across these literatures. Abadie's kappa weights identify moments of the complier population, while instrument-balancing weights are chosen to satisfy covariate-balance conditions. Outcome weights are the realized coefficients on the observed outcomes that reproduce a fitted estimate. They differ from effect weights, which describe how an estimand aggregates heterogeneous causal effects under additional population assumptions \citep{knaus2024treatment}. The present thesis focuses primarily on outcome weights, while instrument-balancing weights enter separately in the assessment of the conditional instrument propensity score.
Taken together, the literature establishes conditional LATE identification, develops normalized and unnormalized kappa estimators, permits flexible nuisance estimation through DML, and provides tools for recovering outcome weights from fitted estimators. The comparative analysis of classical kappa and data-adaptive IV estimators within a common outcome-weight framework remains less developed. This thesis conducts such a comparison while applying the same invariance and finite-sample diagnostics within each empirical IV design.




\subsection{Research question and empirical approach}

Against this background, the central research question is how observation-level outcome weights characterize the finite-sample behavior of classical kappa estimators and data-adaptive IV estimators. Three subsidiary questions structure the analysis. First, do the fitted estimators satisfy the relevant normalization and outcome-invariance properties? Second, do estimators that produce similar point estimates nevertheless differ in effective support, weight concentration, sign cancellation, or covariate balance? Third, how sensitive are these properties to the specification of the instrument propensity score, the choice and tuning of nuisance learners, and the underlying IV design?\\
The empirical analysis compares five kappa estimators with partially linear IV and Wald-AIPW estimators.  Outcome transformations are evaluated at the level of the complete estimation procedure, so that nuisance estimation and hyperparameter tuning are repeated rather than holding a previously fitted weight vector fixed. 
The empirical analysis returns to the three applications studied by
\citet{sloczynski2025abadie}. 
The remainder of the thesis introduces the conditional LATE framework and the estimators, derives their outcome-weight representations and invariance properties, defines the finite-sample diagnostics, presents the three empirical applications, and concludes by synthesizing the cross-application evidence.






 


\section{Econometric Framework}

The econometric analysis is conducted in a binary-instrument, binary-treatment setting with heterogeneous treatment effects. Because treatment selection may be related to potential outcomes, treated and untreated observations are not directly comparable. Instrumental-variable methods instead use treatment variation induced by the instrument. Under treatment-effect heterogeneity, the interpretation of this variation depends on whose treatment status responds to the instrument.
Let $Y_i$ denote the observed outcome, $D_i\in\{0,1\}$ the treatment,
$Z_i\in\{0,1\}$ the instrument, and $X_i$ a vector of pretreatment
covariates.
 Under heterogeneous treatment effects, the resulting estimand depends
on whose treatment status responds to the instrument, which motivates a
potential-outcomes formulation.
Let $D_i(z)$ denote the treatment status that individual $i$ would
receive under instrument value $z\in\{0,1\}$. The observed treatment is
\begin{equation}
    D_i = Z_iD_i(1) + (1-Z_i)D_i(0).
\end{equation}
Before imposing exclusion, let $Y_i(z,d)$ denote the potential outcome
under instrument value $z$ and treatment status $d$. The exclusion
restriction implies
\begin{equation}
    Y_i(1,d)=Y_i(0,d)=Y_i(d), \qquad d\in\{0,1\},
\end{equation}
so that the observed outcome satisfies
\begin{equation}
    Y_i = D_iY_i(1)+(1-D_i)Y_i(0).
\end{equation}

The pair $(D_i(1),D_i(0))$ partitions the population into four latent
compliance types \citep{angrist1996identification}:
\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
 & D_i(0)=0 & D_i(0)=1 \\
\hline
D_i(1)=0 & \text{Never-taker} & \text{Defier} \\
D_i(1)=1 & \text{Complier} & \text{Always-taker}.
\end{array}
\]
Always- and never-takers do not change treatment status with the instrument. Compliers respond in the direction encouraged by the instrument, whereas defiers respond in the opposite direction. Under the monotonicity condition introduced below, defiers are excluded, so that instrument-induced changes in treatment status occur only among compliers. Let $C_i=\mathbf{1}\{D_i(1)>D_i(0)\}$ indicate that individual $i$ is a complier. The corresponding causal parameter is the local average treatment effect \citep{angrist1995identification}
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{\mathrm{LATE}}
    =E\bigl[Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)\mid C_i=1\bigr].
    \label{eq:late_definition}
\end{equation}
This expression defines the target parameter but does not establish its identification from observed data because compliance status and both potential outcomes are unobserved. The applications considered in this thesis allow instrument validity to hold conditional on pretreatment covariates. Under the conditional-IV assumptions stated below, the kappa theorem of \citet{abadie2003semiparametric} recovers moments of the latent complier population from weighted moments of the observed data.



\subsection{Abadie's kappa representation of complier moments}

Let $p(X_i)=P(Z_i=1\mid X_i)$ denote the conditional instrument propensity score. The analysis maintains the conditional-IV assumptions of \citet[Assumption~2.1]{abadie2003semiparametric}, stated here using the notation of \citet[Assumption~IV]{sloczynski2025abadie}.\\
\textbf{Assumptions (Conditional IV).}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[(i)] Independence:
    \[
    \big(Y_i(0,0),Y_i(0,1),Y_i(1,0),Y_i(1,1),D_i(0),D_i(1)\big)
    \perp Z_i \mid X_i.
    \]
    \item[(ii)] Exclusion:
    \[
    Y_i(1,d)=Y_i(0,d) \quad \text{a.s. conditional on } X_i,\quad d\in\{0,1\}.
    \]
    \item[(iii)] First stage and overlap:
    \[
    0<p(X_i)<1,\qquad
    P(D_i(1)=1\mid X_i)>P(D_i(0)=1\mid X_i).
    \]
    \item[(iv)] Monotonicity:
    \[
    P(D_i(1)\geq D_i(0)\mid X_i)=1.
    \]
\end{enumerate}
Condition (i) makes instrument assignment conditionally independent of the
potential variables, while condition (ii) requires the instrument to affect the
outcome only through treatment. Condition (iii) imposes overlap and a positive
first stage in every relevant covariate stratum. Finally condition (iv) excludes
defiers. Under these conditions, \citet[Lemma~2.1]{abadie2003semiparametric} show that, for almost every covariate value $x$,
\begin{align}
    &E[D_i\mid Z_i=1,X_i=x]-E[D_i\mid Z_i=0,X_i=x] \notag\\
    &\qquad=E[D_i(1)-D_i(0)\mid X_i=x]
    =P(C_i=1\mid X_i=x)>0.
    \label{eq:conditional_complier_share}
\end{align}
The conditional first stage therefore identifies the conditional share of compliers. Under monotonicity, always- and never-takers contribute zero to $D_i(1)-D_i(0)$, whereas compliers contribute one. Abadie's main identification result extends this argument from the complier share to general moments of the complier population. Although individual compliance status is unobserved, these moments can be recovered using the following three weighting functions:

\begin{equation}
    \kappa_{0i} = (1-D_i)\,\frac{(1-Z_i)-(1-p(X_i))}{p(X_i)\big(1-p(X_i)\big)}, \qquad
    \kappa_{1i} = D_i\,\frac{Z_i-p(X_i)}{p(X_i)\big(1-p(X_i)\big)},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \kappa_i = \kappa_{0i}\big(1-p(X_i)\big)+\kappa_{1i}\,p(X_i) = 1-\frac{D_i(1-Z_i)}{1-p(X_i)}-\frac{(1-D_i)Z_i}{p(X_i)}.
\end{equation}

The functions $\kappa_i$, $\kappa_{0i}$, and $\kappa_{1i}$ are identification weights. Hence, they may take negative values for observations whose treatment and instrument status differ \citep[p.~237]{abadie2003semiparametric}. Theorem~3.1 of \citet{abadie2003semiparametric} establishes a general representation for moments of the complier population. Since the present analysis focuses on the LATE, only its implications for the two potential-outcome means are required here.
Let
    $\pi_C=P(C_i=1)$
denote the population share of compliers. Specializing Abadie's theorem to the treated and untreated potential outcomes gives
\begin{equation}
    E\bigl[Y_i(1)\mid C_i=1\bigr]
    =
    \frac{E[\kappa_{1i}Y_i]}{\pi_C},
    \qquad
    E\bigl[Y_i(0)\mid C_i=1\bigr]
    =
    \frac{E[\kappa_{0i}Y_i]}{\pi_C}.
    \label{eq:kappa_potential_outcome_means}
\end{equation}
Applying the same result to constant functions yields
\begin{equation}
    \pi_C
    =
    E[\kappa_i]
    =
    E[\kappa_{0i}]
    =
    E[\kappa_{1i}].
    \label{eq:kappa_denominator_equality}
\end{equation}

It follows that the LATE can be expressed as
\begin{align}
    \tau_{\mathrm{LATE}}
    &=
    \frac{E[\kappa_{1i}Y_i]}{E[\kappa_{1i}]}
    -
    \frac{E[\kappa_{0i}Y_i]}{E[\kappa_{0i}]} \notag\\
    &=
    \frac{E[(\kappa_{1i}-\kappa_{0i})Y_i]}{\pi_C},
    \label{eq:kappa_late_representation}
\end{align}
where
\begin{equation}
    \kappa_{1i}-\kappa_{0i}
    =
    \frac{Z_i-p(X_i)}
         {p(X_i)\bigl(1-p(X_i)\bigr)}.
    \label{eq:kappa_outcome_contrast}
\end{equation}

The equality in Equation~\eqref{eq:kappa_denominator_equality} is a population result. The corresponding sample averages generally differ, even when they consistently estimate the same complier share \citep[Remark~2.2]{sloczynski2025abadie}. This distinction gives rise to the alternative kappa estimators introduced next.

\section{Kappa and DML-IV estimators}
\subsection{Kappa-based LATE estimators}
Following \citet[Section~3]{sloczynski2025abadie}, I compare estimators that
differ in how they translate Equation~\eqref{eq:kappa_denominator_equality} into
a sample denominator. The central distinction is normalization. A normalized
estimator rescales each component of an outcome contrast to have weights summing
to one, whereas an unnormalized estimator uses one estimated complier share for both
components. Let $\hat p_i=\hat p(X_i)$ denote the fitted instrument propensity score, and let
$\hat\kappa_i$, $\hat\kappa_{0i}$, and $\hat\kappa_{1i}$ denote the sample
counterparts of the weighting functions defined in the preceding subsection,
obtained by replacing $p(X_i)$ with $\hat p_i$. All estimator formulas below
are evaluated using these fitted quantities.
The first estimator,
$\hat\tau_u$, was proposed by \citet{uysal2011three} and is recommended within
this class by \citet[Section~3.1]{sloczynski2025abadie}. It is the ratio of
separately Hájek-normalized inverse-probability-weighted contrasts for the
effect of $Z_i$ on $Y_i$ and on $D_i$:


\begin{equation}
    \hat\tau_u =
    \frac{
        \left(\sum_{i=1}^N \frac{Z_i}{\hat p_i}\right)^{-1}
        \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{Y_iZ_i}{\hat p_i}
        -
        \left(\sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1-Z_i}{1-\hat p_i}\right)^{-1}
        \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{Y_i(1-Z_i)}{1-\hat p_i}
    }{
        \left(\sum_{i=1}^N \frac{Z_i}{\hat p_i}\right)^{-1}
        \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{D_iZ_i}{\hat p_i}
        -
        \left(\sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1-Z_i}{1-\hat p_i}\right)^{-1}
        \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{D_i(1-Z_i)}{1-\hat p_i}
    }.
    \label{eq:tau_u}
\end{equation}
Thus, the inverse-probability weights are normalized within each instrument
group before either contrast is formed. Appendix~\ref{app:normalization_estimators}
shows the normalization algebra.\\
The second normalized estimator, $\hat\tau_{a,10}$, instead normalizes the
$\hat\kappa_1$- and $\hat\kappa_0$-weighted outcome means separately:
\begin{equation}
    \hat\tau_{a,10} =
    \left(\sum_{i=1}^N \hat\kappa_{1i}\right)^{-1}
    \sum_{i=1}^N \hat\kappa_{1i}Y_i
    -
    \left(\sum_{i=1}^N \hat\kappa_{0i}\right)^{-1}
    \sum_{i=1}^N \hat\kappa_{0i}Y_i.
    \label{eq:tau_a10}
\end{equation}
This estimator has been considered by \citet{abadie2018econometric} and
\citet{sant2022covariate}. Its two sets of component weights each sum to one in
the sample. By contrast, using one of the three sample analogues of the complier
share as a common denominator gives
\begin{align}
    \hat\tau_a
    &=
    \left(N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^N \hat\kappa_i\right)^{-1}
    N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^N
    Y_i\frac{Z_i-\hat p_i}{\hat p_i(1-\hat p_i)},
    \label{eq:tau_a}\\
    \hat\tau_t\;(=\hat\tau_{a,1})
    &=
    \left(N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^N \hat\kappa_{1i}\right)^{-1}
    N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^N
    Y_i\frac{Z_i-\hat p_i}{\hat p_i(1-\hat p_i)},
    \label{eq:tau_t}\\
    \hat\tau_{a,0}
    &=
    \left(N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^N \hat\kappa_{0i}\right)^{-1}
    N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^N
    Y_i\frac{Z_i-\hat p_i}{\hat p_i(1-\hat p_i)}.
    \label{eq:tau_a0}
\end{align}
Under the identifying assumptions and consistent estimation of the instrument
propensity score, all three estimators are consistent for the same population
parameter. In finite samples, however, they use a common estimated complier
share and therefore do not separately normalize the two components of the
outcome contrast.


\subsubsection{Estimating the instrument propensity score}

The applications use two estimators of the instrument propensity score. Both
employ the logistic specification
\[
    F(X_i,\alpha)
    =
    \frac{\exp(X_i'\alpha)}
         {1+\exp(X_i'\alpha)}.
\]
The first estimates $\alpha$ by maximum likelihood and produces
\[
    \hat p_i^{\mathrm{ML}}
    =
    F(X_i,\hat\alpha^{\mathrm{ML}}).
\]
The second applies the covariate-balancing approach of
\citet{imai2014covariate}. The estimate $\hat\alpha^{\mathrm{CB}}$ solves
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N
    \frac{Z_i-\hat p_i^{\mathrm{CB}}}
         {\hat p_i^{\mathrm{CB}}(1-\hat p_i^{\mathrm{CB}})}
    X_i
    =0,
    \qquad
    \hat p_i^{\mathrm{CB}}
    =
    F(X_i,\hat\alpha^{\mathrm{CB}}).
    \label{eq:cbps_moment}
\end{equation}
This condition is equivalent to equality of the inverse-probability-weighted
covariate moments across the two instrument groups. Superscripts
$\mathrm{ML}$ and $\mathrm{CB}$ indicate which fitted propensity score enters
an estimator.\\
When $X_i$ includes a constant, the covariate-balancing condition implies the
exact finite-sample equivalence established by
\citet[Proposition~3.5]{sloczynski2025abadie}:
\begin{equation}
    \hat\tau_u^{\mathrm{CB}}
    =
    \hat\tau_{a,1}^{\mathrm{CB}}
    =
    \hat\tau_{a,0}^{\mathrm{CB}}
    =
    \hat\tau_{a,10}^{\mathrm{CB}},
    \qquad
    \hat\tau_t^{\mathrm{CB}}
    =
    \hat\tau_{a,1}^{\mathrm{CB}}.
    \label{eq:cbps_estimator_equivalence}
\end{equation}
The estimator $\hat\tau_a^{\mathrm{CB}}$ is not covered by this equivalence.
The empirical sections therefore report $\hat\tau_u^{\mathrm{CB}}$ once and
report the remaining distinct kappa estimators using the maximum-likelihood
propensity score.




\subsection{DML-based IV estimators}

The empirical analysis considers two IV estimators constructed within the
double machine learning framework of \citet{chernozhukov2018double}: a
partially linear IV estimator and a Wald augmented inverse-probability-weighted
(Wald-AIPW) estimator. These are the two IV procedures compared with the
classical kappa estimators. Linear and logistic regression, honest forests,
Ranger, and XGBoost enter as alternative learners for the nuisance functions
required by the two scores. The choice of learner therefore changes how the
nuisance functions are estimated, but not the definition of the underlying
estimator.
Following \citet[Sections~2--3]{chernozhukov2018double}, let $W_i$ collect the observed variables, let $\theta_0$ denote a low-dimensional target parameter, and let $\eta_0$ collect the nuisance functions. The target parameter is characterized by the population moment condition
\begin{equation}
    E\!\left[\psi(W_i;\theta_0,\eta_0)\right]=0,
    \label{eq:dml_moment}
\end{equation}
where $\psi(\cdot)$ denotes an estimator-specific score. Directly replacing $\eta_0$ with flexible estimates can make the empirical moment sensitive to regularization error and overfitting. DML addresses this problem by combining a Neyman-orthogonal score with cross-fitting.\\
The score is Neyman orthogonal at $(\theta_0,\eta_0)$ if
\begin{equation}
    \left.
    \frac{\partial}{\partial r}
    E\!\left[
        \psi\bigl(W_i;\theta_0,\eta_0+r(\eta-\eta_0)\bigr)
    \right]
    \right|_{r=0}
    =0
    \label{eq:neyman_orthogonality}
\end{equation}
for admissible perturbations $\eta-\eta_0$. This condition makes the population moment locally insensitive to small first-order perturbations of the nuisance functions. Under the appropriate rate and regularity conditions, nuisance-estimation errors consequently enter through higher-order remainder terms rather than affecting the target moment at first order \citep{chernozhukov2018double}. Orthogonality is a local robustness property and does not eliminate problems
caused by weak instruments, limited overlap, severe nuisance misspecification,
or inaccurate nuisance predictions.
Cross-fitting separates nuisance estimation from score evaluation
\citep[Section~3]{chernozhukov2018double}. Let
$I_1,\ldots,I_K$ denote a partition of the sample into $K$ folds and let
$I_k^c$ denote the corresponding training sample. For each observation
$i\in I_k$, the nuisance functions are estimated using only observations in
$I_k^c$ and are evaluated at $X_i$ to obtain the out-of-fold prediction
$\widehat{\eta}_{-k}(X_i)$. The resulting score contributions are then combined
across folds to estimate the target parameter.
All DML specifications in the empirical analysis use five-fold cross-fitting
and DML2 aggregation \citep[Remark~3.1]{chernozhukov2018double}. The
out-of-fold score contributions are pooled across folds and a single empirical
moment condition is solved. In tuned specifications, hyperparameter selection
is restricted to the corresponding outer training sample, so that observations
in the evaluation fold are used neither for nuisance estimation nor for
tuning.
The precise target parameter, nuisance functions, and orthogonal score differ between PLR-IV and Wald-AIPW. PLR-IV estimates a coefficient defined by a residualized IV moment, whereas Wald-AIPW estimates a ratio of augmented reduced-form and first-stage moments. 



\subsubsection{The partially linear IV estimator}
\label{subsec:pliv}

The first DML procedure considered in this thesis is the partially linear
instrumental-variables (PLR-IV) estimator. Following
\citet[Section~4.2]{chernozhukov2018double}, consider the model
\begin{align}
    Y_i
    &=
    D_i\theta_0+g_0(X_i)+U_i,
    &
    E[U_i\mid X_i,Z_i]
    &=
    0,
    \label{eq:pliv_model}\\
    Z_i
    &=
    m_0(X_i)+V_i,
    &
    E[V_i\mid X_i]
    &=
    0.
    \label{eq:pliv_instrument}
\end{align}
The parameter $\theta_0$ is the constant structural coefficient of interest,
while $g_0(\cdot)$ and $m_0(\cdot)$ are unknown functions. Treatment may be
endogenous because the model does not require
$E[U_i\mid X_i,D_i]=0$. Identification instead uses the component of the
instrument that remains after conditioning on $X_i$.\\
For the partialling-out score, define
$\ell_0(X_i)=E[Y_i\mid X_i]$,
$r_0(X_i)=E[D_i\mid X_i]$, and
$m_0(X_i)=E[Z_i\mid X_i]$. Because the instrument is binary,
$m_0(X_i)=p(X_i)$, the conditional instrument propensity score introduced
above. The orthogonal score is
\begin{equation}
    \psi_{\mathrm{PLR-IV}}(W_i;\theta,\eta)
    =
    \bigl[Y_i-\ell(X_i)-\theta\{D_i-r(X_i)\}\bigr]
    \bigl[Z_i-m(X_i)\bigr],
    \qquad
    \eta=(\ell,r,m).
    \label{eq:pliv_score}
\end{equation}
At the true nuisance functions, this score satisfies both the identifying
moment condition and the Neyman-orthogonality condition. Identification
additionally requires the residualized first stage
$E[(Z_i-m_0(X_i))(D_i-r_0(X_i))]$ to be nonzero. Orthogonality reduces
sensitivity to nuisance-estimation error but does not resolve weak-instrument
problems.\\
Replacing the nuisance functions with the cross-fitted predictions introduced
above gives
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\theta}_{\mathrm{PLR-IV}}
    =
    \frac{
        \sum_{i=1}^{N}
        \{Z_i-\widehat m_{-k(i)}(X_i)\}
        \{Y_i-\widehat\ell_{-k(i)}(X_i)\}
    }{
        \sum_{i=1}^{N}
        \{Z_i-\widehat m_{-k(i)}(X_i)\}
        \{D_i-\widehat r_{-k(i)}(X_i)\}
    },
    \label{eq:pliv_dml_estimator}
\end{equation}
where $k(i)$ denotes the fold containing observation $i$. The estimator can
therefore be interpreted as an IV regression of the residualized outcome on
the residualized treatment using the residualized instrument.\\
The residualized IV moment remains well defined when treatment effects are
heterogeneous, but its estimand should not generally be interpreted as the
unconditional LATE. With a binary instrument, PLR-IV aggregates conditional
LATEs using weights proportional to
$p(X)\{1-p(X)\}\Delta_D(X)$, whereas the unconditional LATE uses weights
proportional to $\Delta_D(X)$, where $\Delta_D(X)$ denotes the conditional
first stage. The two parameters therefore need not coincide when the
instrument propensity and the conditional complier effect vary across
covariate values. Appendix~\ref{app:pliv_late_aggregation} provides the
corresponding derivation. In the empirical analysis, PLR-IV is consequently
treated as a flexible residualized-IV benchmark rather than as a direct
estimator of the same LATE targeted by the kappa and Wald-AIPW estimators.



\subsubsection{The Wald-AIPW estimator}

The second DML procedure is the Wald augmented inverse-probability-weighted
(Wald-AIPW) estimator. Unlike PLR-IV, it does not impose a constant treatment
coefficient. Instead, it estimates the covariate-adjusted effects of the
instrument on the outcome and treatment and forms their ratio. The construction
combines the regression and weighting logic developed by
\citet{tan2006regression} with the orthogonal score and cross-fitted
implementation for the interactive IV model in
\citet[Section~5.2]{chernozhukov2018double}.\\
For the binary instrument and treatment, define
\begin{align}
    g_z(X_i)
    &=
    E[Y_i\mid Z_i=z,X_i],
    && z\in\{0,1\},\\
    r_z(X_i)
    &=
    E[D_i\mid Z_i=z,X_i],
    && z\in\{0,1\},\\
    m(X_i)
    &=
    P(Z_i=1\mid X_i).
    \label{eq:wald_nuisances}
\end{align}
Here, \(g_z\) and \(r_z\) are the instrument-specific outcome and treatment
regressions, while \(m\) is the conditional instrument propensity score. The
covariate-adjusted reduced form and first stage are
\[
    \rho_Y=E[g_1(X_i)-g_0(X_i)],
    \qquad
    \rho_D=E[r_1(X_i)-r_0(X_i)].
\]
Under the conditional-IV assumptions stated above and a positive first stage,
their ratio identifies the LATE:
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{\mathrm{LATE}}
    =
    \frac{\rho_Y}{\rho_D}
    =
    \frac{E[g_1(X_i)-g_0(X_i)]}
         {E[r_1(X_i)-r_0(X_i)]}.
    \label{eq:wald_late_ratio}
\end{equation}
Under monotonicity, \(\rho_D\) equals the population complier share, so this
ratio accommodates heterogeneous treatment effects without imposing the
constant-coefficient model underlying PLR-IV.\\
To obtain an orthogonal representation of the two components, define
\begin{align}
    \Gamma_{Y,i}(\eta)
    ={}&
    g_1(X_i)-g_0(X_i)
    +\frac{Z_i\{Y_i-g_1(X_i)\}}{m(X_i)}
    -\frac{(1-Z_i)\{Y_i-g_0(X_i)\}}{1-m(X_i)},
    \label{eq:wald_gamma_y}\\
    \Gamma_{D,i}(\eta)
    ={}&
    r_1(X_i)-r_0(X_i)
    +\frac{Z_i\{D_i-r_1(X_i)\}}{m(X_i)}
    -\frac{(1-Z_i)\{D_i-r_0(X_i)\}}{1-m(X_i)},
    \label{eq:wald_gamma_d}
\end{align}
where \(\eta=(g_0,g_1,r_0,r_1,m)\). Each pseudo-outcome combines an
instrument-specific regression contrast with an inverse-probability-weighted
residual correction. At the true nuisance functions, their expectations equal
\(\rho_Y\) and \(\rho_D\), respectively. The resulting orthogonal score is
\begin{equation}
    \psi_{\mathrm{Wald-AIPW}}(W_i;\tau,\eta)
    =
    \Gamma_{Y,i}(\eta)-\tau\Gamma_{D,i}(\eta).
    \label{eq:wald_aipw_score}
\end{equation}\\
Each component has a double-robustness property. The reduced form is
consistently estimated if either \(m\) or both outcome regressions are
correctly specified, while the first stage is consistently estimated if
either \(m\) or both treatment regressions are correctly specified.
Consistency of the ratio requires both components to be consistently
estimated and the first-stage denominator to remain nonzero.\\
Let \(\widehat\Gamma_{Y,i}\) and \(\widehat\Gamma_{D,i}\) denote the
pseudo-outcomes evaluated using the corresponding out-of-fold nuisance
predictions. The estimator used in the empirical analysis is
\begin{equation}
    \widehat\tau_{\mathrm{Wald-AIPW}}
    =
    \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\widehat\Gamma_{Y,i}}
         {\sum_{i=1}^{N}\widehat\Gamma_{D,i}}.
    \label{eq:wald_aipw_estimator}
\end{equation}
The inverse-probability corrections require overlap,
\(0<m(X_i)<1\). Estimated propensities close to either boundary can magnify
individual score contributions, an issue examined later through the
overlap and outcome-weight diagnostics. 





\subsubsection{Nuisance-function estimation and tuning}

The empirical analysis uses four learner families for the nuisance functions, which are 
parametric regression, honest forests, random forest, and XGBoost. For
PLR-IV, all three nuisance functions,
$\ell(X)=E[Y\mid X]$, $m(X)=E[Z\mid X]$, and
$r(X)=E[D\mid X]$, are treated as regression problems. The parametric
specification therefore uses linear regression for all three functions.
For Wald-AIPW, the outcome functions $g_z(X)$ are estimated by regression,
whereas the instrument propensity $m(X)$ and treatment functions $r_z(X)$
are estimated as binary classification problems. Its parametric specification
accordingly combines linear outcome regressions with logistic instrument and
treatment models.\\
The headline outcome-weight specifications use honest regression forests from
the generalized-random-forest framework
\citep{athey2019generalized,knaus2024treatment}. These models are fitted
through \texttt{dml\_with\_smoother()}, which also provides the smoother
representations required to recover the corresponding outcome weights.
The alternative learner comparison is implemented through
\textsc{DoubleML} \citep{bach2022doubleml}. It includes fixed parametric and
\texttt{ranger} specifications together with tuned XGBoost specifications
\citep{wright2017ranger,chen2016xgboost}.
The parametric and \texttt{ranger} learners retain their package defaults and
are not subjected to a separate hyperparameter search.\\
For the headline honest forests, setting
\texttt{tune.parameters = "all"} invokes the internal GRF tuning procedure
separately within each outer training sample. The procedure uses out-of-bag
prediction error to select the subsample fraction, number of candidate
variables per split, minimum node size, honesty fraction, honesty pruning rule,
split-balance parameter, and imbalance penalty. Because tuning is conducted
inside the outer training sample, the corresponding evaluation fold does not
enter either nuisance fitting or parameter selection.\\
The XGBoost specifications use fold-specific nested tuning through the
\texttt{mlr3tuning} interface. Within outer fold \(q\),
each nuisance component \(j\) selects
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\lambda}_{j,-q}
    =
    \arg\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{j,q}^{(15)}}
    \frac{1}{3}\sum_{h=1}^{3}
    L_{j,q,h}(\lambda),
    \label{eq:nested_tuning}
\end{equation}
where $\Lambda_{j,q}^{(15)}$ contains 15 configurations drawn by random
search and $L_{j,q,h}$ denotes the prediction loss in inner fold \(h\).
The search varies the number of boosting rounds from 50 to 400, maximum tree
depth from 2 to 5, learning rate from 0.02 to 0.20, and minimum child weight
from 1 to 8.\\
Root mean squared error is used for every PLR-IV nuisance function and for the
Wald-AIPW outcome regressions. Bernoulli log-loss is used for the Wald-AIPW
instrument propensity and treatment regressions. Tuning is performed with
\texttt{tune\_on\_folds = TRUE}, so the selected parameters may differ across
outer folds. The tuned estimator therefore does not generally have one global
hyperparameter vector. Additional software settings, stored-result conventions,
and execution instructions are documented in
\texttt{IMPLEMENTATION.md}.







\section{Outcome Weights, Invariance, and Empirical Diagnostics}
\label{sec:connecting_frameworks}
The preceding chapter introduces estimators with different sample
constructions. The kappa estimators apply explicit functions of the instrument,
treatment, and estimated instrument propensity score, whereas PLR-IV and
Wald-AIPW combine cross-fitted nuisance estimates through orthogonal scores.
Comparing their point estimates reveals whether the estimators disagree in a
given sample, but not how the individual observations contribute to that
disagreement. To make this comparison possible, the analysis uses the pseudo-IV framework of
\citet{knaus2024treatment} to recover an outcome-weight representation for each
fitted estimator,
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\tau}
    =
    \sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_iY_i
    =
    \boldsymbol{\omega}'\boldsymbol{Y},
    \label{eq:outcome_weight_representation}
\end{equation}
where $\omega_i$ is the realized coefficient assigned to observation $i$ and
$\boldsymbol{\omega}=(\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_n)'$ denotes the corresponding
outcome-weight vector.
The recovered weights provide a common basis for classifying the normalization
properties of each realized fit and for examining effective support, weight
concentration, sign cancellation, and covariate balance. Outcome invariance is
evaluated separately by transforming the outcome and rerunning the complete
estimation procedure. 
Throughout the analysis, outcome weights refer to the final sample coefficients
that reproduce the reported estimate. They should not be confused with
Abadie's kappa weights, which are population identification objects used to
recover moments of the complier population.







\subsection{The pseudo-IV representation}
\label{subsec:knaus_framework}

 The pseudo-IV estimator (PIVE) framework of
\citet{knaus2024treatment} provides the construction to represents an
estimator through the empirical moment condition
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}_N\!\left[
        \bigl(\widetilde{Y}_i
        -\widehat{\tau}\widetilde{D}_i\bigr)
        \widetilde{Z}_i
    \right]
    =0,
    \label{eq:pive_moment_thesis}
\end{equation}
where \(\widetilde{Y}_i\), \(\widetilde{D}_i\), and
\(\widetilde{Z}_i\) denote an estimator-specific pseudo-outcome,
pseudo-treatment, and pseudo-instrument. These variables may incorporate
residualization, inverse-probability weighting, augmentation terms, or fitted
nuisance functions.\\
Suppose that the pseudo-outcome admits the representation
\(\widetilde{\mathbf Y}=\mathbf T\mathbf Y\) for a unique transformation
matrix \(\mathbf T\), and that
\(\widetilde{\mathbf Z}'\widetilde{\mathbf D}\neq0\). Proposition~1 of
\citet{knaus2024treatment} then gives the outcome-weight vector as
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \boldsymbol{\omega}'
    =
    \bigl(\widetilde{\mathbf Z}'\widetilde{\mathbf D}\bigr)^{-1}
    \widetilde{\mathbf Z}'\mathbf T.
    }
    \label{eq:pive_outcome_weights_thesis}
\end{equation}
The matrix \(\mathbf T\) describes how the observed outcomes enter the
estimator-specific pseudo-outcome. For the kappa estimators considered here,
it is an explicit diagonal matrix whose elements depend on the instrument,
treatment, and fitted instrument propensity score. Their outcome weights can
therefore be obtained algebraically. For PLR-IV and Wald-AIPW, \(\mathbf T\)
may instead incorporate smoother matrices associated with the fitted outcome
nuisance functions. Whether an exact outcome-weight representation is
available consequently depends on the nuisance learner and its implementation.\\
In the empirical analysis, the kappa outcome weights are constructed directly,
whereas DML outcome weights are recovered whenever the fitted learner provides
the required representation. A candidate vector is treated as exact only if it
reproduces the corresponding estimate through
\(\boldsymbol{\omega}'\mathbf Y=\widehat{\tau}\) to numerical tolerance.
Having established a common and numerically verified representation, the next
step is to compare the normalization properties of the resulting weight
vectors.


\subsection{Normalization classes and the empirical comparison}
\label{subsec:knaus_normalization}


Once \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) has been obtained, its total mass and its masses
within the treated and untreated subsamples provide a common classification of
otherwise different estimators. Following \citet[Table~4]{knaus2024treatment},
define
\begin{equation}
    C=\boldsymbol{\omega}'\mathbf 1_N,
    \qquad
    C_1=\boldsymbol{\omega}'\mathbf D,
    \qquad
    C_0=\boldsymbol{\omega}'(\mathbf 1_N-\mathbf D).
    \label{eq:knaus_weight_masses}
\end{equation}
Because \(C=C_1+C_0\), weights with zero total mass assign equal and opposite
aggregate mass to treated and untreated observations. Knaus refers to weights
with \(C=0\) as normalized. They are \emph{fully normalized} when, in addition,
\begin{equation}
    C_1=1
    \qquad\text{and}\qquad
    C_0=-1.
    \label{eq:knaus_full_normalization}
\end{equation}
In that case, the treated outcomes receive aggregate weight \(+1\), while
the untreated outcomes receive aggregate weight \(-1\), placing the final
contrast on the conventional treated-minus-untreated scale. Individual
weights within either group may nevertheless be negative.
A normalized estimator need not
be fully normalized: it may instead satisfy \(C_1=c\) and \(C_0=-c\) for some
\(c\neq1\). Knaus calls this case scale-normalized. If \(C\neq0\), one or both
sides of the comparison remain unnormalized. In particular, the
untreated-unnormalized class has \(C_1=1\) but \(C_0\neq-1\), whereas the
treated-unnormalized class has \(C_0=-1\) but \(C_1\neq1\). If neither component
has its target mass, the weights are fully unnormalized
\citep[Section~4.1]{knaus2024treatment}.
The complete classification is reported in Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:knaus_weight_classes}, adapted from
\citet[Table~4]{knaus2024treatment}.\\
For DML estimators, these properties may depend on the nuisance learners and
their implementation. Affine outcome smoothers are sufficient for zero total
weight mass, whereas full normalization of PLR-IV and Wald-AIPW requires
additional alignment between the smoother matrices used for the outcome and
treatment nuisance functions
\citep[Sections~4.2.2 and 4.2.4]{knaus2024treatment}. The empirical analysis
therefore calculates \(C\), \(C_1\), and \(C_0\) from every available exact
outcome-weight vector.Because standard PLR-IV and Wald-AIPW implementations generally estimate the
outcome and treatment nuisance functions using separate fitted models, this
alignment condition need not hold. Their outcome weights are therefore
typically scale-normalized rather than fully normalized, although the realized
classification remains implementation-specific
\citep[Sections~4.2.2 and 4.2.4]{knaus2024treatment}.






\subsubsection{Outcome weights of the normalized Uysal estimator}
\label{subsec:omega_u}

The normalized Uysal estimator provides a transparent application of this
classification. \citet[Section~4.2.4]{knaus2024treatment} shows that Wald-IPW
with separately normalized instrument weights is fully normalized and
identifies \(\widehat{\tau}_u\) with this case in Appendix~A.4. To state the
result in the notation used here, define
\begin{equation}
    h_{1i}
    =
    \frac{Z_i/\widehat p_i}
         {\sum_{j=1}^{N}Z_j/\widehat p_j},
    \qquad
    h_{0i}
    =
    \frac{(1-Z_i)/(1-\widehat p_i)}
         {\sum_{j=1}^{N}(1-Z_j)/(1-\widehat p_j)}.
    \label{eq:uysal_normalized_instrument_weights}
\end{equation}
These instrument-group weights satisfy
\(\sum_i h_{1i}=\sum_i h_{0i}=1\). Let
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\Delta}_D^u
    =
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}(h_{1i}-h_{0i})D_i
    \label{eq:uysal_normalized_first_stage}
\end{equation}
denote the corresponding normalized first-stage contrast. Provided that
\(\widehat{\Delta}_D^u\neq0\), applying the PIVE construction gives
\begin{equation}
    \omega_i^u
    =
    \frac{h_{1i}-h_{0i}}
         {\widehat{\Delta}_D^u},
    \qquad
    \widehat{\tau}_u
    =
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}\omega_i^uY_i.
    \label{eq:uysal_outcome_weights_main}
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}\omega_i^u=0,
    \qquad
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}\omega_i^uD_i=1,
    \qquad
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}\omega_i^u(1-D_i)=-1.
    \label{eq:uysal_normalization_main}
\end{equation}
Thus, \(\widehat{\tau}_u\) has fully normalized outcome weights. The complete
PIVE derivation, including the construction of its diagonal transformation
matrix, is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:uysal_outcome_weights}.
Software-specific details on the construction, extraction, and numerical
validation of the outcome-weight vectors are documented in
\texttt{IMPLEMENTATION.md}. With the common representation and normalization
criteria established, the analysis now turns to the connection between
normalization and outcome invariance.




\section{Translation and Logarithmic Scale Invariance}
\label{sec:translation_invariance}

This section examines the translation-invariance property empirically.
Following \citet{sloczynski2025abadie}, the classical kappa estimators are re-estimated after an additive transformation of the outcome. The same test is then applied to the PLR-IV and Wald-AIPW estimators.






\subsection{Definition and empirical outcome transformations}

Translation invariance is the main outcome-transformation property examined in this
thesis. It requires that an estimated treatment effect remain unchanged when the same
constant is added to every observed outcome. This property is emphasized by
\citet{sloczynski2025abadie}, who show that normalized and unnormalized kappa
estimators can behave differently even when they target the same population LATE.
Let \(\mathbf Y\) denote the observed outcome vector and let \(W\) collect all
non-outcome inputs to the estimator. Following
\citet{sloczynski2025abadie}, an estimator is translation invariant if
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\tau}(\mathbf Y+k\mathbf 1_N,W)
    =
    \widehat{\tau}(\mathbf Y,W)
    \label{eq:translation_invariance}
\end{equation}
for every constant \(k\).\\
For an estimator whose outcome weights do not change under the translation,
the outcome-weight representation makes this condition transparent:
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\tau}(\mathbf Y+k\mathbf 1_N)
    -
    \widehat{\tau}(\mathbf Y)
    =
    k\boldsymbol{\omega}'\mathbf 1_N
    =
    kC.
    \label{eq:weight_mass_translation}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:weight_mass_translation} provides a direct explanation for the kappa estimators. Their fitted weights do not depend on the outcome, so an estimator is translation invariant whenever its total outcome-weight mass is zero. If the total mass is nonzero, adding the constant $k$ changes the estimate by $kC$.
For the DML estimators, the fitted outcome nuisance functions may change after the outcome is translated. Checking the total mass of the original fitted weight vector is therefore not sufficient to evaluate the complete procedure. The empirical test consequently reruns the entire estimator using the transformed outcome.
The empirical analysis considers two types of additive outcome transformation.  For a binary 
outcome the original coding
$Y_i\in\{0,1\}$ is replaced by $Y_i+1\in\{1,2\}$. 
Second, this thesis will consider logged monetary
outcomes.
For the logged monetary outcomes, the underlying wage or income variable is converted from dollars to cents before taking logarithms.
\begin{equation}
    \log(100R_i)=\log(R_i)+\log(100).
    \label{eq:dollars_to_cents}
\end{equation}
Both transformations add the same constant to every observation without changing the underlying economic outcome. The monetary transformation is applied to log wages in the Vietnam and Card applications and to maternal log income in the Angrist--Evans application.



\subsection{Empirical assessment of estimator invariance}
\label{subsec:full_pipeline_invariance}

The empirical assessment compares each estimator fitted to the original
outcome with the same estimator fitted to the translated outcome. Let
\(\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{orig}}\) and
\(\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{shift}}\) denote the two resulting estimates. The
reported rerun difference is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{\mathrm{rerun}}
    =
    \widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{shift}}
    -
    \widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{orig}}.
    \label{eq:complete_pipeline_rerun}
\end{equation}
An estimator is classified as translation invariant in the empirical rerun test when
\(\lvert\Delta_{\mathrm{rerun}}\rvert\leq 10^{-4}\).
The classification is based on the unrounded difference, while the tables report rounded values. The threshold is used as a numerical reporting tolerance and is substantially smaller than the three-decimal precision used for the point estimates.\\
For the kappa estimators, the instrument propensity score is re-estimated after the outcome transformation. Because neither the propensity-score model nor its inputs depend on the outcome, the fitted kappa weights should remain unchanged apart from numerical precision. The rerun therefore provides an empirical check of the algebraic translation-invariance result. The comparison is conducted using the original
application samples in the Vietnam and Card designs and the common diagnostic
subsamples in the Angrist--Evans design.\\
For PLR-IV and Wald-AIPW, the complete estimation procedure is repeated. The original and transformed fits use the same sample, covariate specification, learner class, number of cross-fitting folds, tuning rule, tuning budget, and random seed. The seed is reset before each fit so that the same randomization protocol is used. All outcome nuisance functions are estimated again after the transformation. Where tuning is used, hyperparameter selection is also repeated.
For the DML estimators, the reported results refer to the specific learner, tuning procedure, and empirical specification used in each application. Hence, they should not be interpreted as general theoretical statements about all implementations of the corresponding estimator.
Taken together, the outcome-weight representation places the kappa and DML-IV estimators on a common finite-sample basis. The normalization conditions describe the algebraic structure of the realized outcome weights, while the complete-pipeline rerun provides the empirical translation-invariance test after nuisance estimation and tuning are repeated. The following section uses the fitted weights to examine effective support, concentration, sign cancellation, and covariate balance.


\section{Outcome-Weight Diagnostics}
\label{sec:outcome_weight_diagnostics}

The outcome-weight diagnostics characterize the realized sample comparison
underlying each numerically verified estimator. Four complementary properties
are examined. Normalization records the aggregate weight masses, effective
support and maximum absolute weights describe concentration, sign composition
measures cancellation within the treatment components, and covariate balance
characterizes the observed comparison generated by the fitted weights.
Together, these diagnostics distinguish estimators that produce similar point
estimates but use the available observations differently.



\subsection{Normalization}

The empirical tables apply the classification noted in
Section~\ref{subsec:knaus_normalization} to each realized outcome-weight
vector. They report the total mass \(C\) together with the sign-oriented
treated and control masses \(C_1\) and \(-C_0\). A fully normalized estimator
therefore appears as \(C=0\) with component masses \(1/1\), whereas a
scale-normalized estimator has zero total mass but equal component masses that
need not equal one. The classification is calculated from the realized
weights rather than inferred from the estimator name because the
normalization properties of DML estimators can depend on the nuisance learner
and its implementation.

\subsection{Effective support and concentration}
\label{subsec:effective_support}

Normalization describes the aggregate masses of an outcome-weight vector, but
not how those masses are distributed across observations. Two estimators may
belong to the same normalization class while one distributes its weight
broadly and the other assigns much of its absolute weight mass to relatively
few observations. To examine this distinction, I use a modified effective
sample size separately within the treated and untreated components.
For nonnegative weights, the conventional effective sample size of
\citet{kish1965survey} summarizes the loss of effective support associated
with unequal weighting. Because the outcome weights examined here may have
both signs, their signed sum can conceal substantial weight magnitudes through
cancellation. \citet[Section~5.4]{chattopadhyay2023implied} therefore propose
applying the Kish construction to the absolute weights. For treatment state
\(d\in\{0,1\}\), the resulting measure is
\begin{equation}
    ESS_{\mathrm{mod},d}
    =
    \frac{
      \left(
        \sum_i\mathbf 1\{D_i=d\}|\omega_i|
      \right)^2
    }{
      \sum_i\mathbf 1\{D_i=d\}\omega_i^2
    },
    \label{eq:ess_modified_group}
\end{equation}
provided that the denominator is positive.
The use of absolute weights prevents positive and negative coefficients from
cancelling in the numerator. The measure is invariant to a common rescaling
of the weights, lies between one and the number \(N_d\) of observations in
treatment arm \(d\), and reduces to the conventional Kish effective sample
size when the weights are nonnegative. Following
\citet{chattopadhyay2023implied}, it is calculated separately for the treated
and untreated observations because concentration in one component may be
hidden by an overall summary.
To facilitate comparisons across treatment groups and applications, the
empirical tables report
\begin{equation}
    \rho_d
    =
    \frac{ESS_{\mathrm{mod},d}}{N_d},
    \qquad
    N_d=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf 1\{D_i=d\}.
    \label{eq:ess_retention}
\end{equation}
Values of \(\rho_d\) closer to one indicate that the absolute weight
magnitudes are more evenly distributed within treatment arm \(d\), whereas
smaller values indicate greater concentration. The tables additionally report
\(\max_i|\omega_i|\), which records the largest individual coefficient in the
fitted outcome contrast.
These quantities are descriptive diagnostics of the realized weight vector.
They are interpreted jointly with the normalization and sign
diagnostics rather than as standalone measures of estimator quality.



\subsection{Sign composition and cancellation}
\label{subsec:weight_cancellation}

The modified effective sample size deliberately uses absolute weights and
therefore does not distinguish between weight vectors with the same
magnitudes but different signs. Signed outcome weights may nevertheless
contain positive and negative coefficients within both treatment components.
To describe this additional feature, the weights are first placed on a common
treated-minus-control orientation.
Following the descriptive convention used by
\citet[Section~5]{knaus2024treatment}, define
\begin{equation}
    a_i=\omega_i(2D_i-1).
    \label{eq:balance_oriented_weights}
\end{equation}
The treated coefficients remain unchanged, while the control coefficients
are multiplied by minus one. Positive values of \(a_i\) consequently follow
the conventional orientation in both treatment groups.
For each treatment state \(d\), I summarize the opposite-oriented component
by its share of total absolute weight mass:
\begin{equation}
    R_d^{-}
    =
    \frac{
      \sum_i
      \mathbf 1\{D_i=d\}
      |a_i|
      \mathbf 1\{a_i<0\}
    }{
      \sum_i
      \mathbf 1\{D_i=d\}
      |a_i|
    },
    \qquad d\in\{0,1\},
    \label{eq:opposite_sign_mass}
\end{equation}
provided that the denominator is positive. Weighting by \(|a_i|\) ensures
that a large opposite-oriented coefficient contributes more to the statistic
than a coefficient that is negative but numerically negligible.
The statistic is invariant to a common rescaling of the weights and lies
between zero and one. A value of zero means that all absolute weight mass in
the respective treatment component follows the conventional orientation.
Conditional on a positive net oriented mass, values approaching one half
indicate that the positive and negative absolute components are of similar
magnitude and therefore offset substantially. 
Opposite-sign mass is a descriptive property of the realized outcome-weight
vector. It shows
how much internal offsetting accompanies the reported net weight mass and is
therefore interpreted jointly with normalization, effective support, and
maximum weight magnitude. The same oriented weights \(a_i\) are used below to
construct the covariate-balance diagnostics.


\subsection{Covariate balance}
\label{subsec:covariate_balance}

Effective support and sign composition describe the distribution of the
outcome-weight coefficients, but not the observed covariate comparison they
produce. The final diagnostic therefore compares included covariate moments
between the treated and untreated components after applying the 
weights defined in Equation~\eqref{eq:balance_oriented_weights}. This can
reveal whether estimators with similar point estimates construct their fitted
contrasts from different observed covariate comparisons.
For covariate \(X_j\) and treatment state \(d\), define the oriented weighted
mean
\begin{equation}
    \bar X^{\,a}_{dj}
    =
    \frac{
      \sum_i\mathbf 1\{D_i=d\}a_iX_{ij}
    }{
      \sum_i\mathbf 1\{D_i=d\}a_i
    },
    \qquad d\in\{0,1\},
    \label{eq:signed_weighted_mean}
\end{equation}
provided that the denominator is nonzero. Normalizing within each treatment
component makes the resulting comparison invariant to a common rescaling of
the weights in that component.
The balance measure distinguishes continuous and binary covariates. For a
continuous covariate, let \(s_{dj}^2\) denote its unweighted variance in
treatment group \(d\), and define
\[
    s_{j,\mathrm{pool}}
    =
    \left(
      \frac{s_{1j}^2+s_{0j}^2}{2}
    \right)^{1/2}.
\]
The reported absolute mean difference is
\begin{equation}
    B_j(a)
    =
    \begin{cases}
      \displaystyle
      \frac{
        \left|
          \bar X^{\,a}_{1j}-\bar X^{\,a}_{0j}
        \right|
      }{
        s_{j,\mathrm{pool}}
      },
      & X_j \text{ continuous},\\[12pt]
      \left|
        \bar X^{\,a}_{1j}-\bar X^{\,a}_{0j}
      \right|,
      & X_j \text{ binary}.
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:mixed_balance_measure}
\end{equation}
Continuous differences are therefore expressed in units of the pooled
unadjusted standard deviation, while binary differences remain on their raw
zero to one scale. The same continuous denominator is used for the unadjusted
and estimator-weighted comparisons.
Because the oriented weights may be negative, the quantities
\(\bar X^{\,a}_{dj}\) are algebraic signed moments. In particular, a weighted
mean of a binary indicator need not represent a probability or lie between
zero and one. 
The results are displayed using Love plots constructed with the
\texttt{cobalt} package \citep{greifer2024cobalt}, following the application. The reference value of \(0.1\) is used
as a visual guide. It represents one tenth of a pooled standard deviation for
continuous covariates but a raw signed-moment difference of \(0.10\) for
binary covariates and is therefore not interpreted as a common pass--fail
criterion.\\
Taken together, the four diagnostics describe the aggregate scale,
concentration, sign composition, and included covariate moments of each
realized outcome-weight vector. They complement the point estimates and
invariance results but do not by themselves rank estimators or establish the
validity of an empirical IV design. The following sections apply this common
diagnostic sequence to the three frameworks proposed in \citet{sloczynski2025abadie}.

\section{Vietnam Draft Lottery}
\label{sec:vietnam}

The first application revisits \citet{angrist1990lifetime}, who uses Vietnam draft eligibility as an instrument for military service. Eligibility was determined by random sequence numbers and cohort-specific induction ceilings. Because the ceilings differed across birth cohorts, the analysis conditions on age using linear, cubic, and saturated specifications. The sample is the \(N=3{,}027\) subsample of the 1984 Survey of Income and Program Participation also used by \citet{sloczynski2025abadie}. The instrument \(Z_i\) is draft eligibility, the treatment \(D_i\) is veteran status, and the outcome \(Y_i\) is log hourly wage.
The veteran rate is \(40.4\%\) among eligible men and \(26.5\%\) among ineligible men. After conditioning on age, the estimated first stage ranges from \(8.8\) to \(9.5\) percentage points. Fitted instrument propensities range from approximately \(0.14\) to \(0.63\), showing no immediate overlap problem. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:vietnam_descr} and Figure~\ref{fig:vietnam_pscore} report the complete diagnostics.


\subsection{Point Estimates across Age Specifications}

This subsection reports the point estimates obtained using the original log-wage outcome. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:vietnam_point_estimates_age} reports the estimates across the three age specifications. The normalized kappa estimates range from \(0.202\) to \(0.234\) under linear and cubic age adjustment. By contrast, the unnormalized estimates are close to zero under the linear specification, between \(0.014\) and \(0.016\), but range from \(0.302\) to \(0.317\) under cubic adjustment. Under saturated adjustment, all six kappa estimates coincide at \(0.241\). The standard errors are large relative to these differences, so the estimates are used primarily to compare finite-sample estimator behavior rather than to draw precise substantive conclusions.
The DML comparison focuses on the cubic and saturated specifications. Under cubic age adjustment, tuned honest-forest PLR-IV and Wald-AIPW produce estimates of \(0.243\) and \(0.229\), respectively, placing them between the normalized and unnormalized kappa estimates. Under saturated adjustment, both DML estimates are close to the common kappa estimate of \(0.241\). Alternative nuisance learners shift the DML estimates only modestly and do not change this general pattern. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:vietnam_dml_learners} reports the complete learner comparison.


\subsection{Translation Invariance under Complete Re-estimation}
\label{subsec:vietnam_translation}

The Vietnam translation-invariance test converts hourly wages from dollars to cents before taking logarithms, which adds \(\log(100)\) to every observed outcome. Following the procedure defined in Section~\ref{subsec:full_pipeline_invariance}, each estimator is then re-estimated using the transformed outcome.
Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:vietnam_translation_appendix} reports the underlying estimates,
while Table~\ref{tab:vietnam_translation} summarizes the rerun changes.
The kappa estimates reproduce the main classical result. Under cubic age
adjustment, the three normalized kappa estimators are translation invariant,
whereas the three unnormalized estimators are not. For the latter group,
recoding wages in cents changes the estimates by approximately \(0.21\) to
\(0.22\) log points. Under saturated age adjustment, all six kappa estimators
are invariant and coincide at approximately \(0.241\) under both outcome
codings. This agreement is specific to the saturated age-cell specification
in this sample.
The DML exercise focuses on the cubic and saturated specifications used in
the learner comparison. PLR-IV and Wald-AIPW with tuned honest-forest
nuisances are classified as translation invariant under both specifications.
The same result is obtained with regression and \texttt{ranger} nuisance
learners. Thus, for these implementations, re-estimating the complete
procedure after the outcome translation leaves the reported treatment-effect
estimates unchanged under the empirical classification rule.
Tuned XGBoost is the exception. None of the four tuned-XGBoost implementations satisfies the empirical translation-invariance criterion.
The result applies to both PLR-IV and
Wald-AIPW under cubic and saturated age adjustment. The largest rerun change,
approximately \(0.003\), occurs for cubic PLR-IV.
The remaining changes are
smaller and are reported in
Table~\ref{tab:vietnam_translation}. Although these differences are small
relative to the corresponding point estimates, they show that the complete
tuned XGBoost procedures respond to the economically irrelevant change in
the outcome unit.
Overall, the Vietnam results reproduce the distinction between normalized and
unnormalized kappa estimators and show that the same invariance property holds
for most of the implemented DML procedures. The tuned-XGBoost rerun differences are therefore the main exception to the otherwise stable DML results in this application.

\subsection{Outcome-Weight Diagnostics}
\label{subsec:vietnam_weights}
The outcome-weight analysis is restricted to vectors that reproduce their corresponding estimates numerically. The XGBoost reconstructions are therefore excluded, as is the cubic PLR-IV reconstruction with OLS nuisances. Under cubic age adjustment, the normalized kappa estimators are fully normalized, while the three unnormalized estimators reproduce their expected classes. The verified DML representations are generally scale-normalized.

Linear PLR-IV and linear/logit Wald-AIPW are also fully normalized, whereas the forest implementations remain scale-normalized.\\

Across both age specifications, modified ESS ratios remain above \(0.89\), while approximately \(43\%\)–\(44\%\) of treated and \(46\%\)–\(47\%\) of control absolute weight mass has the opposite orientation. Under saturated adjustment, all eligible weighted differences remain below approximately \(0.03\), and the corresponding plot is therefore omitted.
Overall, the age specification changes the normalization properties more strongly than it changes effective support or sign composition. The balance results additionally show that full normalization does not guarantee exact covariate balance, as the CBPS construction balances age more closely than several fully normalized MLE-based representations.

\section{College Proximity and Returns to Schooling}
\label{sec:card}

The second empirical application revisits \citet{card1993using}, who uses proximity to a four-year college as an instrument for schooling. Following \citet{sloczynski2025abadie}, the analysis uses \(N=3{,}010\) men from the National Longitudinal Survey of Young Men. The outcome \(Y_i\) is log hourly wage, and the instrument \(Z_i\) indicates whether the individual grew up near a four-year college in 1966.
In the following, two binary treatment definitions are considered. The first is schooling beyond high school and the second refers to completion of at least sixteen years of schooling.
Because these definitions represent different education margins, they correspond to different causal parameters. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:card_design_appendix} provides the exact variable definitions.
The analysis compares the broader Card conditioning set with the more parsimonious Kitagawa specification based on \citet{kitagawa2015test}. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:card_covariates_appendix} reports the included covariates.
For schooling beyond high school, the adjusted first stage is \(6.4\) percentage points under the Card specification and \(6.5\) percentage points under Kitagawa. At the sixteen-year margin, it falls to \(3.0\) and \(3.8\) percentage points, with robust \(t^2\) values of only \(3.5\) and \(3.9\). This limited first-stage information makes the corresponding ratio estimates particularly sensitive to small changes in the reduced form or first-stage denominator.
The fitted instrument propensities are bounded away from zero, but their upper tail reveals an asymmetric support concern. Under the Card specification, \(40\) observations with \(Z_i=0\) have \(\hat p(X_i)>0.90\), compared with \(9\) under Kitagawa. No observation with \(Z_i=1\) has \(\hat p(X_i)<0.10\) under either specification. Limited support is therefore concentrated among individuals who have a high fitted probability of living near a college but are observed with \(Z_i=0\). Taken together, the sixteen-year treatment margin and the richer Card conditioning set form the most empirically demanding design cells in this application.



\subsection{Estimates across Treatment Margins and Conditioning Sets}
\label{subsec:card_estimates}


Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:card_point_estimates_appendix} and Table~\ref{tab:card_selected_estimates_appendix} report the point estimates using the original log-wage outcome.
The 2SLS estimates for schooling beyond high school are \(0.661\) under the Card specification and \(0.575\) under Kitagawa. At the sixteen-year margin, they increase to \(1.392\) and \(0.991\), with standard errors of \(0.801\) and \(0.611\).
Moreover, because the treatment definitions represent different schooling margins, their magnitudes should not be interpreted as evidence that one education level has a larger return. \citet{sloczynski2025abadie} additionally caution that exclusion or monotonicity may fail in this application. The estimates are consequently interpreted primarily as evidence on estimator behavior.
The normalized kappa estimates are comparatively stable across conditioning sets. For schooling beyond high school, they range from \(0.293\) to \(0.376\), while at the sixteen-year margin they range from \(0.586\) to \(0.853\). The unnormalized estimators are considerably more sensitive. For example, \(\widehat{\tau}^{ml}_{t}\) changes from \(0.171\) under the Card specification to \(0.769\) under Kitagawa for schooling beyond high school, and from \(0.319\) to \(1.367\) at the sixteen-year margin. The conditioning-set sensitivity is therefore concentrated primarily among the unnormalized kappa estimators.
The DML estimates cluster more clearly by score than by nuisance learner. PLR-IV generally lies closer to 2SLS, whereas Wald-AIPW lies closer to the normalized kappa estimates. This comparison is descriptive because PLR-IV need not target the same unconditional LATE. The separation between the scores is most visible at the weaker sixteen-year margin.
XGBoost provides the clearest tuning sensitivity. The untuned Wald-AIPW estimates are negative in all four design cells and lie far from the remaining results. After fold-specific tuning, they range from \(0.238\) to \(0.730\), placing them within the broader range of the other DML and normalized kappa estimates. Tuning changes PLR-IV less strongly. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:card_xgb_tuning_appendix} reports the complete comparison.


\subsection{Translation Invariance under Complete Re-estimation}
\label{subsec:card_translation}

The CARD translation-invariance test converts hourly wages from dollars to cents before taking logarithms and repeats the complete estimation procedure in all four design cells.
Table~\ref{tab:card_translation} reports the resulting estimate changes, while Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:card_translation_kappa_appendix} and~\ref{tab:card_translation_dml_appendix} provide the underlying estimates.
The kappa results reproduce the normalization distinction. All three normalized estimators remain unchanged, whereas the unnormalized estimators change substantially in every design cell. Their absolute changes range from approximately \(0.44\) to \(0.92\) under the Card specification and from \(1.28\) to \(4.53\) under Kitagawa. The direction and size of the change therefore depend on the realized normalization failure and conditioning set, but the invariance classification is common across the four cells.\\
Among the DML estimators, linear PLR-IV and linear/logit Wald-AIPW satisfy the \(10^{-4}\) criterion under both conditioning sets and treatment definitions. The forest implementations show a systematic conditioning-set pattern. Honest forest and \texttt{ranger} exceed the tolerance under the richer Card specification but remain within it under Kitagawa. Because this result appears across both DML scores and forest implementations, it is consistent with greater numerical sensitivity under the richer conditioning set, although it does not identify which component of that specification produces the difference.
Tuned XGBoost exceeds the tolerance in all eight comparisons. PLR-IV changes more under the Card specification, whereas Wald-AIPW changes more under Kitagawa. The largest DML change is \(0.0218\), obtained for Card PLR-IV at the sixteen-year margin. More generally, the nonparametric implementations produce larger changes at the weaker sixteen-year margin, although this does not alter their classifications.
Overall, the parametric DML implementations satisfy the empirical invariance criterion in all four design cells. Forest-based invariance depends on the conditioning set, while tuned XGBoost exceeds the tolerance throughout. The DML changes nevertheless remain considerably smaller than those of the unnormalized kappa estimators.

\subsection{Outcome-Weight Normalization and Concentration}
\label{subsec:card_weight_diagnostics}

The outcome-weight analysis is restricted to vectors that reproduce their corresponding fitted estimates. All eligible DML representations pass this check, while XGBoost is excluded because its reconstructed weights do not reproduce the estimates.
Across all four design cells, linear PLR-IV is fully normalized, whereas linear/logit Wald-AIPW and both forest implementations are scale-normalized. The classification is therefore stable across treatment definitions and conditioning sets. The common treated and sign-oriented control mass nevertheless varies across cells. For tuned-forest Wald-AIPW at the sixteen-year margin, for example, it equals \(0.9442\) under the Card covariates and \(1.0072\) under Kitagawa.\\
The concentration diagnostics reveal a stronger difference between PLR-IV and Wald-AIPW. Within each design cell and nuisance specification, Wald-AIPW has a lower modified ESS and a larger maximum absolute weight than the corresponding PLR-IV estimator.
This difference is largest under the Card covariates at the sixteen-year margin. For the tuned honest forest, the treated and control ESS ratios are \(0.398\) and \(0.466\) for Wald-AIPW, compared with \(0.578\) and \(0.615\) for PLR-IV. The maximum absolute weight simultaneously rises from \(0.055\) to \(0.277\). The Wald-AIPW outcome contrast is therefore more concentrated among influential observations in this design cell. Opposite-sign mass remains comparatively stable, indicating that concentration rather than sign composition accounts for the main difference between the two representations. Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:card_weights_card_somecol}--\ref{tab:card_weights_kit_educ16} report the complete diagnostics.
Appendix Figures~\ref{fig:card_balance_card} and~\ref{fig:card_balance_kitagawa} examine covariate balance. The CBPS-based kappa representation removes the included covariate differences in all four cells, consistent with the balancing restrictions imposed during propensity-score estimation. The forest and MLE-kappa weights substantially reduce the large initial differences in experience and experience squared under the Card specification, although residual imbalance is generally greater at the sixteen-year margin. Adjustment does not improve every covariate separately: some initially small differences in regional indicators move away from zero after weighting. The normalized and unnormalized MLE-kappa variants have essentially identical balance profiles because group-specific rescaling changes their aggregate masses but not the corresponding weighted covariate means.
 Overall, the CARD application combines the weakest first stage, the clearest support concern, and the greatest outcome-weight concentration among the three applications. Estimator differences are most pronounced in the empirically demanding cells, particularly for Wald-AIPW at the sixteen-year margin under the richer Card conditioning set.

\section{Fertility and Mothers' Labor-Market Outcomes}
\label{sec:child}


The third application revisits the fertility design of \citet{angrist1996children}, which uses the sex composition of the first two children as an instrument for additional fertility. Following \citet{sloczynski2025abadie}, the analysis uses the 1980 U.S. Census sample constructed by \citet{farbmacher2018increasing}. The population consists of women aged 21–35 with at least two children. The treatment \(D_i\) indicates whether a woman has more than two children, while the instrument \(Z_i\) indicates whether her first two children have the same sex. Parents with two boys or two girls are, on average, more likely to have another child than parents with one boy and one girl.
Two outcomes are examined. Work status indicates whether the woman worked for pay in the preceding year. Maternal log income is defined among women with strictly positive reported annual income. The common conditioning set contains mother’s age, age at first birth, first-child sex, and indicators for Black, Hispanic, and other race.
 Second-child sex is not included because,
together with first-child sex, it deterministically reveals the instrument and
creates exact collinearity within the instrument-specific nuisance regressions.
This restriction is unlikely to drive the conventional IV results.
\citet{angrist1996children} for instance report that their principal female labor-supply
coefficients change by no more than approximately two percent when the additive
child-sex controls are excluded. Appendix~\ref{app:child_covariate_design} provides the complete variable definitions and specification details.\\
The work-status parent sample contains \(394{,}840\) women, while the positive-income parent sample contains \(220{,}502\). Because the full Census samples make repeated machine-learning and outcome-weight calculations computationally demanding, the analysis uses one fixed proportional-stratified diagnostic subsample of \(3{,}000\) observations for each outcome.
The raw first stages in these subsamples, \(0.0606\) for work status and \(0.0530\) for maternal income, are close to the corresponding parent-sample values of \(0.0595\) and \(0.0543\).
Appendix~\ref{app:subsampling} documents the sampling procedure and Appendix Table~\ref{tab:ae_descriptive_diagnostics} reports the complete comparison.
After adjustment for the common covariates, the first stages are \(0.0648\) and \(0.0596\). Fitted instrument propensities range from approximately \(0.45\) to \(0.55\) in the work-status sample and from \(0.46\) to \(0.57\) in the maternal-income sample. The application therefore combines strong propensity-score overlap with a modest first stage and reliance on two fixed diagnostic subsamples.





\subsection{Point Estimates and Learner Sensitivity}
\label{subsec:child_estimates}


Appendix Table~\ref{tab:ae_child_estimates_appendix} reports the conventional IV, kappa, and DML estimates. For work status, 2SLS and all normalized and unnormalized kappa estimates are approximately \(-0.095\).
Normalization therefore has almost no numerical effect at the original outcome coding. The DML estimates have the same negative direction but range from approximately \(-0.101\) to \(-0.163\) across nuisance learners. Their standard errors range from approximately \(0.26\) to \(0.28\), which is large relative to the differences between the point estimates.
The variation is therefore interpreted as learner sensitivity rather than as evidence of substantively different effects.
For maternal log income, 2SLS and the normalized kappa estimators produce estimates of approximately \(-0.182\), while the unnormalized estimates are close to \(-0.177\). Most DML estimates lie between \(-0.214\) and \(-0.263\). The main exception is tuned-XGBoost Wald-AIPW at \(-0.341\). Standard errors range from approximately \(0.72\) to \(0.79\), again making the estimates too imprecise to support strong substantive comparisons.
XGBoost displays the clearest tuning sensitivity. The untuned Wald-AIPW estimates are \(1.177\) for work status and \(4.386\) for maternal log income, with standard errors of \(2.291\) and \(9.650\). After fold-specific tuning, the estimates become \(-0.117\) and \(-0.341\), placing them substantially closer to the remaining DML estimates. Tuning also moves PLR-IV closer to the other results, although less dramatically. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:ae_child_xgb_tuning_appendix} reports the complete comparison.








\subsection{Translation Invariance under Complete Re-estimation}
\label{subsec:child_translation}

The translation-invariance exercise shifts work status from \(0/1\) to \(1/2\) and converts maternal income from log dollars to log cents. These are the transformations defined in Section~\ref{sec:translation_invariance}. Each estimator is then re-estimated using the transformed outcome. Table~\ref{tab:child_translation} reports the resulting changes.
The classical estimators reproduce the normalization distinction. The 2SLS estimate and all three normalized kappa estimators remain unchanged for both outcomes, whereas the three unnormalized kappa estimators change. The changes are small in these diagnostic samples, amounting to approximately \(0.0004\) for work status and \(0.0025\) for maternal log income. Normalization consequently has little effect on the original point estimates but determines whether the estimates remain unchanged after the additive outcome transformation.
Among the DML implementations, linear PLR-IV and linear/logit Wald-AIPW remain unchanged to numerical precision. The \texttt{ranger} implementations produce only small departures. For work status, PLR-IV lies slightly above the \(10^{-4}\) tolerance, while Wald-AIPW remains within it. Both maternal-income implementations exceed the tolerance, but their absolute changes remain below \(0.001\).
The tuned honest-forest implementations show clearer departures, with absolute changes between approximately \(0.0023\) and \(0.0061\).

Although tuning moves the XGBoost point estimates closer to the remaining estimates, it does not make the complete estimation pipeline translation invariant.
Overall, the normalized kappa and parametric DML implementations satisfy the empirical invariance criterion for both outcomes. The \texttt{ranger} departures remain small, while tuned honest forest and XGBoost show greater implementation sensitivity under complete re-estimation.

\subsection{Outcome-Weight Diagnostics}
\label{subsec:child_weight_diagnostics}

When it comes to Outcome Weight diagnostics both outcomes, the normalized kappa estimators are fully normalized. Among the unnormalized estimators, \(\widehat{\tau}_{a}\) is fully unnormalized, \(\widehat{\tau}_{t}\) is untreated-unnormalized, and \(\widehat{\tau}_{a,0}\) is treated-unnormalized. They do not imply precise estimation and do not contradict the large standard errors reported above. Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:child_weights_work_appendix} and~\ref{tab:child_weights_income_appendix} provide the complete weight masses, extrema, ESS measures, and sign-composition results.\\

Only the PLR-IV value lies marginally above the \(0.1\) reference value. The corresponding \texttt{ranger} differences remain below \(0.043\).

his pattern is not shared by \texttt{ranger}, for which every difference remains below \(0.071\). Overall, the Angrist–Evans application combines strong instrument-propensity overlap and broadly distributed weights with substantial sign cancellation and considerable statistical imprecision. Its main diagnostic result is that high effective support does not guarantee close covariate balance: the honest-forest income representations have ESS ratios close to one but retain substantial imbalance in age at first birth.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Empirical Designs and Cross-Application Evidence}
\label{subsec:cross_application}

The three applications provide distinct empirical settings for examining the
finite-sample behavior of the estimators. In the Vietnam application, draft
eligibility is generated by the lottery conditional on age, and the relevant
conditioning problem is limited to alternative representations of a single
design variable. Together with its comparatively informative first stage,
this provides the most transparent setting in the analysis. The Card design
is more demanding because college proximity is observational, instrument
validity depends on a richer set of individual and regional characteristics,
and the results can be examined under two conditioning sets and two treatment
margins. First-stage information is particularly limited at the sixteen-year
margin. The Angrist--Evans application presents a different configuration.
The fitted instrument propensities indicate strong overlap, but the first
stage remains modest and the computational analysis relies on two fixed
diagnostic subsamples. The applications therefore differ in instrument
assignment, first-stage strength, covariate complexity, and sample
construction. These differences provide complementary settings in which to
assess whether the finite-sample behavior of the estimators remains stable
when the empirical design becomes more demanding in different dimensions.
The point estimates already reveal that estimator sensitivity is not uniform
across these settings. In the Vietnam application, the estimates become
comparatively stable under the richer cubic and saturated age specifications,
with only limited differences across the main DML implementations. In the
Card application, the estimates separate more clearly by DML score: PLR-IV
generally remains closer to the conventional IV benchmark, whereas Wald-AIPW
is closer to the normalized kappa estimates. These differences are most
visible at the sixteen-year treatment margin, which also contains the least
first-stage information, although this correspondence does not establish that
the weaker first stage causes the divergence. In the Angrist--Evans
application, the normalized and unnormalized kappa estimators produce very
similar estimates under the original outcome coding, while the DML results
display moderate variation across nuisance learners. XGBoost provides the
clearest implementation sensitivity in the Card and Angrist--Evans
applications: its default specifications produce estimates far from the
remaining results, whereas tuning moves them substantially closer to the
other DML estimates. Taken together, the applications show that agreement in
the reported point estimates can arise under quite different empirical
conditions and does not imply that the estimators share the same
finite-sample weighting behavior.


\subsection{Normalization, Invariance, and Outcome-Weight Diagnostics}
\label{subsec:diagnostic_synthesis}

The comparison of point estimates does not reveal whether the estimators
construct the same finite-sample outcome contrast. The normalization and
translation-invariance results provide the first distinction. The three
normalized kappa estimators are invariant under every outcome transformation
considered in the applications. Whenever the realized weights of the
unnormalized kappa estimators remain unnormalized, their estimates change
after the outcome translation. The saturated Vietnam specification is an
important exception: its age-cell structure makes all six kappa estimators
numerically equivalent and fully normalized, so that the nominally
unnormalized estimators are also invariant in that specification. Outside
this special case, the magnitude of non-invariance differs considerably
across applications. The changes are pronounced under the cubic Vietnam and
Card specifications but much smaller in the Angrist--Evans samples. The small
Angrist--Evans changes do not alter the formal classification; they show
instead that the practical magnitude of non-invariance depends on the
realized empirical design.
The DML results add a distinction that is not visible from the classical
estimators. Among the eligible representations, linear PLR-IV provides the
clearest case of full normalization. It is fully normalized in the Card and
Angrist--Evans applications and under saturated age adjustment in Vietnam.
Most of the remaining verified DML representations are scale-normalized. Parametric DML is nevertheless the most consistently
translation-invariant implementation when the complete procedure is
re-estimated. The forest results depend more strongly on the empirical
setting. Honest forests and \texttt{ranger} are invariant in Vietnam, become
sensitive under the Card conditioning set, and show learner-specific
departures in the Angrist--Evans application. Tuned XGBoost is the least
stable implementation across the complete-pipeline reruns. These findings
show why normalization and translation invariance should not be treated as
interchangeable properties for data-adaptive estimators. Algebraic
normalization describes the realized outcome-weight representation, whereas
complete-pipeline invariance additionally reflects how nuisance estimation,
cross-fitting, and tuning respond to the transformed outcome.\\
The effective-support diagnostics reveal a second cross-application
difference. The Vietnam and Angrist--Evans weights are distributed across a
large proportion of their respective treatment groups. The Card application
produces substantially greater concentration, particularly for Wald-AIPW at
the weaker sixteen-year treatment margin. Under the Card covariates, the
honest-forest Wald-AIPW representation has treated and control ESS ratios of
\(0.398\) and \(0.466\), compared with \(0.578\) and \(0.615\) for
honest-forest PLR-IV. Its maximum absolute weight is also considerably larger.
The Wald-AIPW estimate in this cell consequently depends more strongly on a
smaller set of influential observations.  Effective support therefore adds information
that neither the point estimate nor the normalization class provides.\\
Covariate balance identifies a further dimension of the realized comparison.
The CBPS-based kappa estimator produces the most systematic exact balance of
the included covariates, reflecting the moment conditions used to estimate
its instrument propensity score. Full normalization alone does not produce
the same result. In Vietnam, for example, some fully normalized MLE-kappa
representations retain larger age differences than the CBPS representation.
Conversely, a high effective sample size does not guarantee close balance.
The clearest example is maternal log income in the Angrist--Evans
application. The honest-forest representations have treated and control ESS
ratios close to one, but retain standardized differences of \(0.509\) for
PLR-IV and \(0.461\) for Wald-AIPW in age at first birth. The corresponding
\texttt{ranger} representations have slightly lower ESS ratios but keep every
covariate difference below \(0.071\). Thus, the learner that distributes
absolute weight more evenly need not construct the more closely balanced
covariate comparison. Concentration and covariate balance describe distinct
properties of the outcome weights and need not rank estimators in the same
order.
Finally, opposite-sign mass is substantial in all three applications but
generally varies less across estimators than effective support and maximum
weight magnitude. It therefore explains comparatively little of the
cross-estimator differences found in the Card and Angrist--Evans
applications. The diagnostic remains informative because a weight vector can
be broadly distributed while still containing considerable offsetting
between positive and negative coefficients. This is particularly visible in
the Angrist--Evans application, where ESS ratios close to one coexist with
opposite-sign shares close to one half. These shares should not be interpreted
as a ranking of estimator quality. They describe the degree of internal sign
cancellation accompanying the realized outcome contrast.

\subsection{Nuisance Learning and Practical Implications}
\label{subsec:learner_implications}

The cross-application evidence also illustrates how nuisance-learning choices
enter the finite-sample behavior of DML estimators. Linear PLR-IV and
linear/logit Wald-AIPW are the most numerically stable implementations in the
translation exercises. Their estimates remain unchanged when the complete
procedure is repeated after transforming the outcome. This stability is
particularly valuable in low-dimensional settings where a parametric
description of the nuisance functions is plausible. It does not, however,
establish that the underlying linear and logistic models are correctly
specified. Parametric stability should therefore be interpreted as an
implementation advantage rather than as evidence that parametric nuisance
models are universally preferable to more flexible alternatives.
The two forest implementations must also be distinguished. Honest forests
serve as the headline outcome-weight specification because their fitted
smoother matrices permit a direct examination of the conditions underlying
the recovered weights. The \texttt{ranger} specifications instead provide an
alternative random-forest implementation through \textsc{DoubleML}. Although
both approaches use tree-based nuisance learning, their finite-sample
behavior is not interchangeable. In the Card application, both forest
families are sensitive to the richer Card conditioning set but remain close to
invariant under the more parsimonious Kitagawa specification. The
Angrist--Evans income results reveal a different distinction. The
honest-forest representations distribute their weights very broadly but
retain substantial imbalance in age at first birth. The \texttt{ranger}
representations achieve better balance in the same sample, although their
weights are slightly more concentrated. This does not establish a general
ranking in favor of \texttt{ranger}. Instead, it shows that changing a
prediction-oriented nuisance learner can improve one property of the realized
outcome contrast while weakening another.
XGBoost displays the strongest implementation sensitivity. In the Card and
Angrist--Evans applications, the default Wald-AIPW specifications produce
estimates far from the remaining results, while the default PLR-IV estimates
also show notable departures in the Angrist--Evans samples. Fold-specific
tuning moves these estimates substantially closer to the other DML and kappa
results. Nevertheless, the tuned procedures remain comparatively sensitive
when the complete estimation pipeline is repeated after translating the
outcome. Moreover, the extracted XGBoost weight vectors do not reproduce the
corresponding fitted estimates under the numerical identity check. They
therefore cannot support conclusions about normalization, effective support,
sign cancellation, or covariate balance. In this thesis, XGBoost provides
evidence on point-estimate and tuning sensitivity, but not a validated
outcome-weight representation.
These findings suggest a diagnostic procedure rather than a universally
preferred estimator. Normalized kappa estimates provide a transparent
invariance benchmark, while alternative DML scores and nuisance learners show
whether the empirical conclusions depend on a particular implementation.
Normalization, complete-pipeline invariance, concentration, sign cancellation,
and covariate balance should then be evaluated separately because success in
one dimension does not imply success in the others. Hyperparameter tuning
must likewise be treated as part of the implemented estimator rather than as
an incidental computational choice. Within this comparison,
\(\widehat{\tau}^{CB}_{u}\) provides the most transparent finite-sample
benchmark because it combines full normalization, translation invariance, and
systematic balance of the included covariates. These properties do not make it
universally superior, nor do they establish instrument validity, but they make
its realized outcome comparison particularly transparent.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:limitations}

Several limitations qualify the interpretation of the results. First, the
outcome-weight diagnostics are descriptive properties of the realized fitted
estimators. Effective sample size and maximum weight magnitude describe the
concentration of the outcome contrast, while the balance statistics summarize
differences in the included observed covariates. These diagnostics can reveal
potentially influential observations or unexpected comparisons, but they
cannot establish instrument validity, balance unobserved characteristics, or
identify the causal estimand without the required IV assumptions.\\
Second, the DML results are implementation-specific. The reported estimates
and invariance classifications depend on the selected learner families,
cross-fitting structure, tuning budgets, random seeds, and software
implementations. Using fixed seeds makes the comparisons reproducible within
the analysis, but it does not establish stability across alternative sample
splits or tuning searches. The results should therefore be interpreted as
evidence on the implemented procedures rather than as general properties of
PLR-IV, Wald-AIPW, or the respective learner classes.\\
Third, the Angrist--Evans analysis is based on one fixed diagnostic subsample
of \(3{,}000\) observations for each outcome rather than on the complete
Census samples or repeated subsamples. Proportional stratification preserves
the principal characteristics of the parent samples, but the reported DML and
outcome-weight patterns may still depend on the realized draws. In addition,
the maternal-income analysis is restricted to women with positive reported
income. Its results therefore describe this selected outcome sample and
should not be interpreted as an unconditional effect on maternal income.\\
Finally, the reconstructed XGBoost weight vectors do not reproduce the
corresponding fitted estimates under the numerical identity check. This
prevents the XGBoost specifications from entering the normalization,
concentration, sign-cancellation, and covariate-balance analysis. The failure
concerns the weight extraction available for the particular implementations
used here. It should not be interpreted as a general theoretical failure of
boosting methods or as evidence that XGBoost estimators cannot possess valid
outcome-weight representations under a different implementation.




\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This thesis examined what observation-level outcome weights reveal about the
finite-sample behavior of classical kappa estimators and
machine-learning-assisted IV estimators. Its main contribution was to combine
the outcome-weight framework with complete-pipeline translation tests in
which nuisance estimation, cross-fitting, and tuning are repeated after the
outcome is transformed. This makes it possible to distinguish the algebraic
properties of a realized weight vector from the stability of the implemented
estimation procedure.
Three conclusions emerge from the empirical analysis. First, normalization
provides a transparent explanation for the invariance of the classical kappa
estimators, although the numerical importance of non-normalization depends on
the empirical design. Second, the behavior of DML estimators cannot be inferred
from their score or learner class alone. It depends on the interaction between
the nuisance specification, tuning procedure, and empirical setting. Third,
similar point estimates do not imply similar finite-sample comparisons.
Estimators that agree numerically can differ in normalization, effective
support, weight concentration, and covariate balance. These diagnostics
therefore provide complementary information and should not be interpreted as
alternative rankings of estimator quality.
Overall, the thesis shows that the comparison of IV estimators should not end
with their reported coefficients. Outcome weights make the realized empirical
contrast explicit, while complete-pipeline reruns reveal whether this contrast
remains stable under economically irrelevant outcome transformations.
Together, these tools provide a more informative assessment of classical and
data-adaptive IV estimators in finite samples.

\section{Normalization of the Weighting Estimators}
\label{app:normalization_estimators}

\subsection{Normalization of \texorpdfstring{$\widehat{\tau}_u$}{tau-u}}
Define the normalized inverse-probability weights for observations with $Z_i=1$ and $Z_i=0$, respectively, as
\begin{equation}
h_{1i}
=
\frac{Z_i/p(X_i)}
{\sum_{j=1}^N Z_j/p(X_j)},
\qquad
h_{0i}
=
\frac{(1-Z_i)/(1-p(X_i))}
{\sum_{j=1}^N (1-Z_j)/(1-p(X_j))}.
\label{eq:hajek_weights}
\end{equation}
By construction,
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i=1}^N h_{1i}
=
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^N Z_i/p(X_i)}
{\sum_{j=1}^N Z_j/p(X_j)}
=1,
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i=1}^N h_{0i}
=
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^N (1-Z_i)/(1-p(X_i))}
{\sum_{j=1}^N (1-Z_j)/(1-p(X_j))}
=1.
\end{equation}
Using these weights, define the normalized weighted means
\begin{align}
\widehat{\mu}_{Y,1}
&=
\sum_{i=1}^N h_{1i}Y_i,
&
\widehat{\mu}_{Y,0}
&=
\sum_{i=1}^N h_{0i}Y_i, \\
\widehat{\mu}_{D,1}
&=
\sum_{i=1}^N h_{1i}D_i,
&
\widehat{\mu}_{D,0}
&=
\sum_{i=1}^N h_{0i}D_i.
\end{align}
The estimator in Equation~\eqref{eq:tau_u} can therefore be written compactly as
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\tau}_u
=
\frac{
\widehat{\mu}_{Y,1}-\widehat{\mu}_{Y,0}
}{
\widehat{\mu}_{D,1}-\widehat{\mu}_{D,0}
}.
\label{eq:tau_u_compact}
\end{equation}
Thus, $\widehat{\tau}_u$ is a weighted Wald estimator in which both the reduced-form contrast and the first-stage contrast are constructed from separately normalized inverse-probability-weighted means.
The reduced-form numerator may also be written as a single weighted sum:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mu}_{Y,1}-\widehat{\mu}_{Y,0}
=
\sum_{i=1}^N (h_{1i}-h_{0i})Y_i.
\end{equation}
The corresponding signed outcome weights sum to zero:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i=1}^N(h_{1i}-h_{0i})
=
\sum_{i=1}^N h_{1i}
-
\sum_{i=1}^N h_{0i}
=
1-1=0.
\label{eq:tau_u_zero_sum}
\end{equation}
Hence, although the weights within each component sum to one, the signed weights entering their difference sum to zero.


\subsection{Normalization of \texorpdfstring{$\widehat{\tau}_{a,10}$}{tau-a10}}
The estimator $\widehat{\tau}_{a,10}$ is given by
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\tau}_{a,10}
=
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^N\kappa_{1i}Y_i}
{\sum_{i=1}^N\kappa_{1i}}
-
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^N\kappa_{0i}Y_i}
{\sum_{i=1}^N\kappa_{0i}}.
\end{equation}
Define the normalized component weights
\begin{equation}
a_i
=
\frac{\kappa_{1i}}
{\sum_{j=1}^N\kappa_{1j}},
\qquad
b_i
=
\frac{\kappa_{0i}}
{\sum_{j=1}^N\kappa_{0j}}.
\label{eq:kappa_normalized_weights}
\end{equation}
Provided that the respective denominators are nonzero, these weights satisfy
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i=1}^N a_i
=
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^N\kappa_{1i}}
{\sum_{j=1}^N\kappa_{1j}}
=1,
\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^N b_i
=
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^N\kappa_{0i}}
{\sum_{j=1}^N\kappa_{0j}}
=1.
\end{equation}
Accordingly,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\tau}_{a,10}
=
\sum_{i=1}^N a_iY_i
-
\sum_{i=1}^N b_iY_i.
\label{eq:tau_a10_weighted_means}
\end{equation}
The estimator can equivalently be expressed as a single signed weighted sum:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\tau}_{a,10}
=
\sum_{i=1}^N(a_i-b_i)Y_i.
\end{equation}
Defining
\begin{equation}
\omega_i=a_i-b_i,
\end{equation}
the signed outcome weights satisfy
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i=1}^N\omega_i
=
\sum_{i=1}^N a_i
-
\sum_{i=1}^N b_i
=
1-1=0.
\label{eq:tau_a10_zero_sum}
\end{equation}
It is therefore important to distinguish between two statements. First, the weights used in each component of $\widehat{\tau}_{a,10}$ sum separately to one. Second, once the two components are combined into a treatment-effect contrast, the resulting signed outcome weights sum to zero. 

\section{PLR-IV as an aggregation of conditional Wald estimands}
\label{app:pliv_late_aggregation}

The difference between the PLR-IV estimand and the unconditional LATE can be
made explicit when the instrument is binary. Let
\[
    p(X)=P(Z=1\mid X)
\]
and define the conditional reduced form and first stage as
\begin{align}
    \Delta_Y(X)
    &=
    E[Y\mid Z=1,X]-E[Y\mid Z=0,X],\\
    \Delta_D(X)
    &=
    E[D\mid Z=1,X]-E[D\mid Z=0,X].
\end{align}
Since
\[
E[(Z-p(X))Y\mid X]
=
p(X)\{1-p(X)\}\Delta_Y(X)
\]
and
\[
E[(Z-p(X))D\mid X]
=
p(X)\{1-p(X)\}\Delta_D(X),
\]
the residualized IV estimand can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \theta_{\mathrm{PLR-IV}}
    =
    \frac{E[p(X)\{1-p(X)\}\Delta_Y(X)]}
         {E[p(X)\{1-p(X)\}\Delta_D(X)]}.
    \label{eq:pliv_conditional_wald_aggregation}
\end{equation}

Under the conditional IV assumptions and monotonicity,
\(\Delta_D(X)\) equals the conditional complier share. Whenever
\(\Delta_D(X)>0\), the conditional Wald ratio
\[
    \tau_C(X)
    =
    \frac{\Delta_Y(X)}{\Delta_D(X)}
\]
identifies the conditional LATE. Equation
\eqref{eq:pliv_conditional_wald_aggregation} can therefore be rewritten as
\begin{equation}
    \theta_{\mathrm{PLR-IV}}
    =
    \frac{
      E[p(X)\{1-p(X)\}\Delta_D(X)\tau_C(X)]
    }{
      E[p(X)\{1-p(X)\}\Delta_D(X)]
    }.
\end{equation}
By contrast, the unconditional LATE is
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{\mathrm{LATE}}
    =
    \frac{
      E[\Delta_D(X)\tau_C(X)]
    }{
      E[\Delta_D(X)]
    }.
\end{equation}
Thus, PLR-IV attaches additional weight to covariate regions in which the
instrument propensity is close to one half. The two aggregations generally
differ when \(p(X)\) and the conditional complier effect vary across
covariate values. They coincide, for example, when the conditional LATE is
constant or when \(p(X)\) is constant across \(X\).

\section{Outcome-Weight Derivations for the Kappa Estimators}
\label{app:normalization_estimators}


\subsection{PIVE derivation for the normalized Uysal estimator}
\label{app:uysal_outcome_weights}

This subsection specializes the PIVE construction of
\citet{knaus2024treatment} to the normalized estimator proposed by
\citet{uysal2011three}. Section~4.2.4 of \citet{knaus2024treatment} establishes
the full normalization of Wald-IPW under separate normalization of the
instrument weights, while Appendix~A.4 identifies \(\widehat{\tau}_u\) with
this case. The derivation below records the corresponding observation-level
outcome weights in the notation used in this thesis. Let \(\widehat p_i=\widehat p(X_i)\) and define the
instrument-group normalizing constants
\begin{equation}
    s_1=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{Z_j}{\widehat p_j},
    \qquad
    s_0=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1-Z_j}{1-\widehat p_j}.
    \label{eq:u_s1_s0}
\end{equation}
The corresponding normalized instrument inverse-probability weights are
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_{1i}^{z,n}
    =\frac{N Z_i/\widehat p_i}{s_1},
    \qquad
    \lambda_{0i}^{z,n}
    =\frac{N(1-Z_i)/(1-\widehat p_i)}{s_0}.
    \label{eq:u_norm_lambda}
\end{equation}
Both vectors have total mass \(N\), that is,
\(\sum_i\lambda_{1i}^{z,n}=\sum_i\lambda_{0i}^{z,n}=N\). This is precisely
the separate H\'ajek normalization imposed on the two instrument groups in
\(\widehat\tau_u\).

Define the diagonal matrix
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf T^u
    =\operatorname{diag}\!\left(
      \lambda_{11}^{z,n}-\lambda_{01}^{z,n},\ldots,
      \lambda_{1N}^{z,n}-\lambda_{0N}^{z,n}
    \right).
    \label{eq:u_T_matrix}
\end{equation}
Then the normalized reduced form and first stage in
Equation~\eqref{eq:tau_u} can be collected in the ratio
\begin{equation}
    \widehat\tau_u
    =\frac{N^{-1}\mathbf 1_N'\mathbf T^u\mathbf Y}
           {N^{-1}\mathbf 1_N'\mathbf T^u\mathbf D}.
    \label{eq:u_wald_matrix}
\end{equation}
Thus, \(\widehat\tau_u\) is a PIVE with
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{\mathbf Y}=\mathbf T^u\mathbf Y,
    \qquad
    \widetilde{\mathbf D}=\mathbf T^u\mathbf D,
    \qquad
    \widetilde{\mathbf Z}=\mathbf 1_N.
    \label{eq:u_pive_components}
\end{equation}
No outcome-regression smoother is required: conditional on the fitted
propensity scores, \(\mathbf T^u\) is an explicit diagonal transformation that
does not depend on \(\mathbf Y\). This applies to both the maximum-likelihood
and CBPS versions of the estimator used in the empirical analysis.

For compactness, denote the normalized IPW first stage by
\begin{align}
    \widehat\Delta_D^u
    &:=N^{-1}\mathbf 1_N'\mathbf T^u\mathbf D \notag\\
    &=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}Z_jD_j/\widehat p_j}{s_1}
      -\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}(1-Z_j)D_j/(1-\widehat p_j)}{s_0}.
    \label{eq:u_delta_scalar}
\end{align}
Provided that \(s_1\) and \(s_0\) are finite and nonzero and that
\(\widehat\Delta_D^u\neq0\), Equation~\eqref{eq:pive_outcome_weights_thesis}
gives
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol\omega^{u\prime}
    =\left(N^{-1}\mathbf 1_N'\mathbf T^u\mathbf D\right)^{-1}
      N^{-1}\mathbf 1_N'\mathbf T^u
    =\left(\widehat\Delta_D^u\right)^{-1}
      N^{-1}\mathbf 1_N'\mathbf T^u.
    \label{eq:u_omega_matrix}
\end{equation}
Consequently, the final coefficient attached to observation \(i\)'s outcome is
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \omega_i^u
    =\frac{1}{\widehat\Delta_D^u}
      \left[
        \frac{Z_i}{\widehat p_i s_1}
        -\frac{1-Z_i}{(1-\widehat p_i)s_0}
      \right].}
    \label{eq:u_omega_scalar}
\end{equation}
It follows immediately that
\(\widehat\tau_u=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\omega_i^uY_i\). Each coefficient is therefore the observation's signed normalized instrument
weight divided by the common normalized first-stage contrast.

Under these existence conditions, the three outcome-weight masses follow
directly from the separate instrument-group normalization. First,
the equal total mass of the two normalized instrument-weight vectors implies
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}\omega_i^u
    =\frac{1}{\widehat\Delta_D^u}
      \left(\frac{1}{s_1}\sum_i\frac{Z_i}{\widehat p_i}
            -\frac{1}{s_0}\sum_i\frac{1-Z_i}{1-\widehat p_i}\right)
    =0.
    \label{eq:u_omega_sum}
\end{equation}
Second, the definition of \(\widehat\Delta_D^u\) yields
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}\omega_i^uD_i=1.
    \label{eq:u_omega_treated_sum}
\end{equation}
Finally,
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}\omega_i^u(1-D_i)
    =\sum_i\omega_i^u-\sum_i\omega_i^uD_i=-1.
    \label{eq:u_omega_untreated_sum}
\end{equation}
Hence \(\widehat\tau_u\) is fully normalized in the classification of
\citet{knaus2024treatment}: its total outcome-weight mass is zero, while the
treated and untreated masses are exactly \(+1\) and \(-1\). Importantly, this
result concerns the final outcome weights, not merely the two inverse-probability
weight vectors appearing in the estimator's numerator.

\citet{uysal2011three} proposed the normalized estimator, while
\citet{sloczynski2025abadie} study its finite-sample properties.
\citet[Table~3, Section~4.2.4, and Appendix~A.4]{knaus2024treatment}
identifies \(\widehat\tau_u\) with the normalized Wald-IPW case and shows that
normalization of the instrument IPW components yields fully normalized outcome
weights. The derivation above specializes this general result to the notation
of the kappa estimators and records the corresponding observation-level
coefficient explicitly. It thereby provides the exact weight vector used in
the subsequent normalization, balance, effective-sample-size, and
weight-concentration diagnostics.
The precise role of the separate H\'ajek normalization relative to
\(\widehat\tau_{a,1}\), including the special case in which covariate
balancing already equalizes the two instrument-weight masses, is discussed in
Appendix~\ref{app:omega_a1}, Remark~\ref{rem:hajek_contrast}.









\subsection{Outcome Weights of \texorpdfstring{$\hat\tau_{a,1}$}{tau(a,1)}}
\label{app:omega_a1}

This section derives the outcome weights of $\hat\tau_{a,1}$ (also written $\hat\tau_t$
in \citealt{sloczynski2025abadie}) in the PIVE framework of
\citet{knaus2024treatment}. For a generic propensity-score estimate that does
not impose the required balancing equality, the estimator is
untreated-unnormalized.

\paragraph{Setup.}
Define the raw instrument IPW contrast for observation $i$ as
\begin{equation}
  r_i = \frac{Z_i}{\hat{p}_i} - \frac{1-Z_i}{1-\hat{p}_i}
      = \frac{Z_i - \hat{p}_i}{\hat{p}_i(1-\hat{p}_i)}.
  \label{eq:a1_ri}
\end{equation}
The two kappa components $\kappa_{i1}$ and $\kappa_{i0}$ decompose $r_i$ as follows:
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_{i1} = D_i \frac{Z_i - \hat{p}_i}{\hat{p}_i(1-\hat{p}_i)} = D_i r_i,
  \qquad
  \kappa_{i0} = (1-D_i)\frac{(1-Z_i)-(1-\hat{p}_i)}{\hat{p}_i(1-\hat{p}_i)}
              = -(1-D_i)r_i.
  \label{eq:a1_kappa_decomp}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:a1_kappa_decomp} is the central identity: the treated part of $r_i$
is $\kappa_{i1}$ and the untreated part is $-\kappa_{i0}$.

\paragraph{PIVE representation.}\mbox{}\\
Define the diagonal transformation matrix
$\mathbf{T}^{a,1} = \mathrm{diag}(r_1, \ldots, r_N)$.
Using \eqref{eq:a1_kappa_decomp}, the pseudo-treatment is
$\mathbf{T}^{a,1}\mathbf{D} = \boldsymbol{\kappa}_1$,
and the estimator can be written as
\begin{equation}
  \hat\tau_{a,1}
  = \frac{N^{-1}\mathbf{1}_N'\mathbf{T}^{a,1}\mathbf{Y}}
         {N^{-1}\mathbf{1}_N'\mathbf{T}^{a,1}\mathbf{D}}
  = \frac{N^{-1}\sum_i r_i Y_i}{N^{-1}\sum_i \kappa_{i1}},
  \label{eq:a1_wald}
\end{equation}
which is a PIVE with $\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}} = \mathbf{1}_N$,
$\widetilde{\mathbf{D}} = \boldsymbol{\kappa}_1$,
$\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}'\widetilde{\mathbf{D}} = \sum_i\kappa_{i1}$.
Applying Equation~\eqref{eq:pive_outcome_weights_thesis}, the outcome weights are
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\omega}^{a,1\prime}
  = \left(\sum_i\kappa_{i1}\right)^{-1} \mathbf{1}_N' \mathbf{T}^{a,1},
  \label{eq:a1_omega_vec}
\end{equation}
so that the weight of observation $i$ is
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
    \omega_i^{a,1} = \frac{r_i}{\sum_j \kappa_{j1}}
    = \frac{1}{\sum_j \kappa_{j1}}
      \cdot\frac{Z_i - \hat{p}_i}{\hat{p}_i(1-\hat{p}_i)}.
  }
  \label{eq:a1_omega_scalar}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Normalization properties.}

\noindent\textit{(i) Total weight sum.}
Let $s_1 = \sum_i Z_i/\hat{p}_i$ and $s_0 = \sum_i(1-Z_i)/(1-\hat{p}_i)$.
Then $\sum_i r_i = s_1 - s_0$, so
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{i=1}^N \omega_i^{a,1}
  = \frac{\sum_i r_i}{\sum_j\kappa_{j1}}
  = \frac{s_1 - s_0}{\sum_j\kappa_{j1}},
  \label{eq:a1_sum}
\end{equation}
Thus, $\sum_i\omega_i^{a,1}=0$ if and only if $s_1=s_0$. This equality is
not imposed by a generic propensity-score estimator, so
$\sum_i\omega_i^{a,1}\neq0$ in general. The CBPS exception relevant for this
thesis is stated in Remark~\ref{rem:hajek_contrast} below.
\noindent\textit{(ii) Treated weight sum.}
Using $D_i r_i = \kappa_{i1}$ from \eqref{eq:a1_kappa_decomp}:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{i=1}^N \omega_i^{a,1} D_i
  = \frac{\sum_i D_i r_i}{\sum_j\kappa_{j1}}
  = \frac{\sum_i \kappa_{i1}}{\sum_j\kappa_{j1}}
  = 1. \qquad\checkmark
  \label{eq:a1_treated}
\end{equation}
The treated weights sum to one exactly, because the denominator of $\hat\tau_{a,1}$
is $\sum_i\kappa_{i1}$ — precisely the treated-side kappa total.

\noindent\textit{(iii) Untreated weight sum.}
Using $(1-D_i)r_i = -\kappa_{i0}$ from \eqref{eq:a1_kappa_decomp}:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{i=1}^N \omega_i^{a,1}(1-D_i)
  = \frac{\sum_i (1-D_i) r_i}{\sum_j\kappa_{j1}}
  = \frac{-\sum_i \kappa_{i0}}{\sum_j\kappa_{j1}},
  \label{eq:a1_untreated}
\end{equation}
which equals $-1$ only if $\sum_i\kappa_{i0}=\sum_i\kappa_{i1}$. A generic
propensity-score estimate does not enforce this finite-sample equality, and
therefore $\sum_i\omega_i^{a,1}(1-D_i)\neq-1$ in general.

\paragraph{Classification.}
Collecting the three results:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{i=1}^N \omega_i^{a,1} \neq 0,
  \qquad
  \sum_{i=1}^N \omega_i^{a,1} D_i = 1,
  \qquad
  \sum_{i=1}^N \omega_i^{a,1}(1-D_i) = -\frac{\sum_i\kappa_{i0}}{\sum_i\kappa_{i1}} \neq -1.
  \label{eq:a1_classification}
\end{equation}
Therefore, in the generic case, $\hat\tau_{a,1}$ is
\emph{untreated-unnormalized} in the sense of$\sum_i\kappa_{i0}$ for the two sides, forcing both to sum to their target values;
$\hat\tau_{a,1}$ uses only the treated-side denominator, leaving the untreated side
without its own rescaling.
\citet[Table~4]{knaus2024treatment}: the treated side is normalized by construction
but the untreated side is not. The contrast with $\hat\tau_{a,10}$ is instructive:
the normalized estimator uses separate denominators $\sum_i\kappa_{i1}$ and
$\sum_i\kappa_{i0}$ for the two sides, forcing both to sum to their target values;
$\hat\tau_{a,1}$ uses only the treated-side denominator, leaving the untreated side
without its own rescaling.

\begin{remark}[Role of the separate H\'ajek normalization]
\label{rem:hajek_contrast}
Equation~\eqref{eq:a1_sum} shows that the total outcome-weight mass of
\(\widehat\tau_{a,1}\) remains proportional to the difference
\(s_1-s_0\). By contrast, \(\widehat\tau_u\) normalizes the two instrument
groups separately, so Equation~\eqref{eq:u_omega_sum} reduces its total mass
to \((\widehat\Delta_D^u)^{-1}(1-1)=0\). For this pair of Wald-IPW
estimators, separate H\'ajek normalization is therefore the algebraic step that
guarantees translation invariance without requiring an additional balancing
restriction.

This comparison changes when the propensity-score procedure itself imposes
that restriction. With an intercept included in the CBPS balancing equation
\eqref{eq:cbps_moment},
\begin{equation}
    s_1-s_0
    =\sum_{i=1}^{N}
      \frac{Z_i-\widehat p_i}
           {\widehat p_i(1-\widehat p_i)}
    =0
    \label{eq:cbps_equal_ipw_masses}
\end{equation}
up to numerical tolerance. In this case,
\(\widehat\tau_{a,1}^{cb}\) also has zero total outcome-weight mass and
coincides with \(\widehat\tau_u^{cb}\), consistently with the finite-sample
equivalence discussed in Chapter~3.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Numerical difference in the cubic CBPS replication}
\label{subsubsec:vietnam_cbps_replication}

The only difference from the published table is the cubic-age
\(\hat{\tau}_{u}^{cb}\) estimate. Both implementations use the same sample,
covariates, estimator, and sandwich variance formula. The difference arises
from the numerical solution of the CBPS balancing conditions in a poorly
conditioned cubic polynomial. The Stata solution underlying the published
value has a maximum absolute balancing residual of
\(5.45\times10^{-4}\), whereas the standardized R solver reduces this
residual to \(4.41\times10^{-14}\). Supplying the Stata propensity scores to
the R estimator reproduces both the published estimate and its standard
error.

\subsection{Covariate Specification and Instrument Construction}
\label{app:child_covariate_design}

The common conditioning set used by all kappa and DML estimators is
\[
X_i=
\left(
\text{age}_i,
\text{agefirst}_i,
\text{boy}_{1i},
\text{Black}_i,
\text{Hispanic}_i,
\text{OtherRace}_i
\right).
\]
The definitions of these variables are the following:

\label{tab:ae_child_covariates}
\small
\begin{tabular}{ll}

Covariate & Definition \\
\midrule

Second-child sex remains part of the underlying data and is used to construct
the instrument, but it is not included in the conditioning set \(X_i\). In
particular,
\[
Z_i
=
1\{\text{boy}_{1i}=\text{boy}_{2i}\}.
\]
The relationship has two implications for the nuisance estimation. First,
within each instrument arm, the two child-sex indicators are perfectly
collinear. When \(Z_i=1\), the indicators are identical, whereas when \(Z_i=0\),
one equals one minus the other. Including both indicators together with an
intercept therefore creates exact multicollinearity parametric
nuisance regressions.\\
Second, a flexible learner supplied with both child-sex indicators can
reconstruct \(Z_i\) deterministically. Conditional on both indicators,
\[
P(Z_i=1\mid X_i)\in\{0,1\},
\]
which eliminates the conditional instrument variation required by the overlap
condition and by the DML nuisance regression for \(E[Z_i\mid X_i]\). This
problem does not appear as a numerical collinearity problem in conventional
pooled additive 2SLS because the two child-sex indicators enter only as
separate main effects, while reconstructing the same-sex instrument requires
their interaction.



\subsection{Subsampling Procedure}
\label{app:subsampling}

The diagnostic samples are constructed by proportional stratified sampling
without replacement. Let \(Z_i\) denote the same-sex instrument and \(D_i\)
the indicator for having more than two children. In the labor-force parent
sample, the strata are defined by \(Z_i\), \(D_i\), labor-force participation,
positive-income status, a four-category race variable, and mother's age group
(21--25, 26--30, or 31--35). In the positive-income sample, labor-force
participation and positive-income status are replaced by income decile.\\
For a parent sample containing \(N_0\) observations, let \(N_s\) denote the
number of observations in stratum \(s=1,\ldots,S\). Given the target sample
size \(n=3{,}000\), the proportional allocation is
\[
n_s^\ast=n\frac{N_s}{N_0}.
\]
The initial integer allocation is \(\lfloor n_s^\ast\rfloor\). Remaining
observations are assigned to the strata with the largest fractional remainders
until the allocations sum to \(3{,}000\). The allocated observations are then
drawn uniformly without replacement within each stratum. Finally, the labor and income samples are drawn separately using seed 202.
